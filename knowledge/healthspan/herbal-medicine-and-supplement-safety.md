# Herbal medicine and supplement safety

## Scope

Plants contain pharmacologically active compounds and can produce benefit, adverse effects, interactions, allergy, toxicity, or no meaningful effect. “Natural,” traditional, culinary, organic, or available without prescription does not establish safety, efficacy, purity, or suitability.

VladOS may support evidence review and general safety education. It must not diagnose, prescribe herbal treatment, recommend replacing medication, manage poisoning, or imply competence as a herbalist, pharmacist, toxicologist, or clinician.

## Evidence distinctions

Keep these layers separate:

1. **Traditional/historical use:** establishes cultural and historical practice, not modern efficacy or safety.
2. **Botanical identification and chemistry:** establishes constituents or plausible mechanisms, not patient outcomes.
3. **Preclinical evidence:** laboratory/animal findings generate hypotheses and may reveal hazards; they do not establish human benefit.
4. **Human observational evidence:** may show associations but remains vulnerable to confounding and product variability.
5. **Controlled clinical evidence:** evaluate the exact preparation, dose, population, comparator, outcomes, duration, and harms.
6. **Current guidance and pharmacovigilance:** govern live safety, interaction, contraindication, and regulatory use.

An evidence result for one extract cannot automatically be transferred to teas, powders, tinctures, essential oils, whole plants, different species/parts, or mixed products.

## Product identity and quality

Record the scientific name, plant part, preparation/extraction, concentration or extract ratio, dose, route, duration, manufacturer, batch, jurisdiction, and independent quality information. Common names are ambiguous. Risks include species substitution, wrong plant part, adulteration with drugs, contaminants, pesticides, microbes, allergens, heavy metals, variable potency, and inaccurate labels.

Wild harvesting and home preparation add identification, dose, contamination, conservation, and legal risks. Essential oils and concentrated extracts are not interchangeable with culinary use and can be toxic by ingestion or other routes.

## Interaction and vulnerability gate

Professional review is especially important with prescription or non-prescription medicines, multiple products, surgery/procedures, pregnancy or breastfeeding, children, older adults, liver/kidney/cardiovascular/neurological disease, bleeding risk, allergy, cancer treatment, transplantation, or immune-modifying care.

Check both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions, including effects on bleeding, sedation, seizure threshold, blood pressure, glucose, serotonin, cardiac rhythm, liver enzymes, kidney function, and immune activity. Absence from an interaction database does not prove safety.

## Adaptogens and broad wellness categories

“Adaptogen,” “detox,” “tonic,” “immune boosting,” “hormone balancing,” and similar labels are broad commercial or traditional categories, not diagnoses or guaranteed mechanisms. Any claim about stress, mood, fatigue, cognition, immunity, hormones, inflammation, or disease needs exact-product and population-specific evidence plus safety review.

## Adverse events and escalation

Stop non-essential use and obtain appropriate advice when a suspected adverse response occurs. Severe allergic symptoms, breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure, severe confusion, chest symptoms, uncontrolled bleeding, marked jaundice, or suspected poisoning require local emergency services or poison-control guidance. Do not induce vomiting or improvise a remedy unless instructed by an appropriate service.

Preserve the package/product, ingredient list, dose/timing, batch, co-medications, symptoms, and actions for clinical assessment and adverse-event reporting.

## Failure modes

- Turning traditional use or constituent chemistry into an efficacy claim.
- Recommending an herb without exact identity, preparation, dose, duration, and interaction review.
- Treating a consumer recipe, materia medica, practitioner textbook, or testimonial as a current guideline.
- Assuming a database's silence, a low dose, culinary history, or “natural” label proves safety.
- Using herbs to replace urgent care, diagnosis, disease-modifying treatment, psychiatric care, or prescribed medication.
- Publishing universal adaptogen, detox, immune, hormone, or cure claims.
