# Facilitation and learning design

## Distinctions

- A meeting exchanges or coordinates information.
- A facilitated session helps a group think, decide, align, or create together.
- A workshop combines structured participation with a defined work product.
- Training builds capability through practice, feedback, retrieval, and transfer.
- A gathering also carries relational, cultural, inclusion, and hosting responsibilities.

Do not use “workshop” as a label for a presentation followed by questions.

## Design principles

1. Start with the performance, decision, or artifact needed after the session—not the slide deck.
2. Diagnose whether the gap is knowledge, skill, environment, incentives, authority, motivation, conflict, or missing evidence. Training cannot repair every gap.
3. Contract purpose, participants, decision rights, confidentiality, constraints, success criteria, and follow-through before designing activities.
4. Prepare evidence before the room. Use workshop time for sense-making, choice, rehearsal, and commitment rather than avoidable information transfer.
5. Separate divergence from convergence. Generate without premature evaluation, then select using explicit criteria and visible tradeoffs.
6. Balance voice and power through silent generation, small groups, structured turns, accessible formats, and transparent decision rules.
7. Prefer realistic practice, retrieval, feedback, spacing, and application over passive exposure and “learning style” matching.
8. Make outputs operational: owner, action, date, dependency, measure, review cadence, and escalation path.
9. Evaluate beyond satisfaction. Track whether participants can perform, transfer the behavior, and produce the intended organizational result.

## Facilitation stance

The facilitator owns process quality, not the group's substantive answer. Neutrality does not mean ignoring harmful behavior, hidden power, false information, or a sponsor manipulating a pre-decided outcome. Name the decision method, surface dissent, distinguish facts from assumptions, and protect meaningful participation without promising equality that the organization will not honor.

## Learning mechanisms

- Cognitive load: reduce extraneous complexity and sequence unfamiliar material.
- Retrieval: ask participants to recall or produce, not merely reread.
- Practice and feedback: simulate the target behavior with observable criteria.
- Spacing and interleaving: revisit learning across time and contexts.
- Prior knowledge: activate relevant mental models and repair misconceptions.
- Transfer: include job-realistic cues, tools, rehearsal, manager support, and follow-up.
- Reflection: consolidate insight, while avoiding reflection as a substitute for action.

## Failure modes

- Vague purpose, excessive agenda, wrong participants, or no decision owner.
- Sponsor asks for participation but has already chosen the answer.
- Brainstorming without research, criteria, feasibility, or implementation.
- Dominant voices, hierarchy, language, disability, culture, or remote access suppress participation.
- Activities selected for novelty rather than outcome.
- Facilitation compresses conflict into artificial consensus.
- Decisions leave the room without owners, resources, records, or review.
- Evaluation stops at enjoyment or attendance.

## Evidence limit

The Workshops library is primarily practitioner literature plus applied learning-science syntheses. Its principles should be treated as strong design heuristics and tested against context and outcomes, not marketed as guaranteed workshop impact.
