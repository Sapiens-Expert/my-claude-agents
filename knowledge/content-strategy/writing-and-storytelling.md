# Writing and storytelling

## Useful writing sequence

1. Name the reader, situation, desired understanding/action, and evidence burden.
2. Reduce the piece to one controlling idea.
3. Put the useful conclusion or tension early.
4. Structure information in the reader’s likely order of questions.
5. Use concrete nouns, active verbs, short paragraphs, useful headings, examples, and explicit transitions.
6. Replace abstraction and jargon with observable consequences.
7. Add evidence proportional to claim strength and risk.
8. Revise for meaning, structure, voice, precision, and mechanics—in that order.

## Story model

A story becomes useful when a person in a recognizable situation wants something, encounters meaningful change or resistance, makes choices, and reaches an outcome or unresolved decision. For educational content:

- the audience or client pattern—not the brand—carries the central problem;
- causality and uncertainty must not be simplified beyond the evidence;
- status, conflict, emotion, and surprise may create attention but cannot manufacture facts;
- a personal story demonstrates experience, not universal efficacy;
- client stories require consent, de-identification, and non-typicality safeguards.

## Persuasive copy model

- Clarify the audience and relevant problem.
- Translate features/mechanisms into concrete, evidence-supported consequences.
- Show proof and limitations before asking for commitment.
- Reduce legitimate friction and answer objections honestly.
- Make the next step specific, proportionate, reversible where possible, and easy to decline.

Direct-response books supply headline, benefit, proof, offer, and CTA patterns. Fear, scarcity, belonging, authority, curiosity, and social proof may be discussed only when real and relevant; they must never be fabricated or weaponized.
