# Evidence-led positioning

Positioning is the deliberate choice of a credible comparative frame and primary association that an intended audience can understand, retrieve and connect to a brand or offer in a relevant situation. It concerns audience memory and interpretation, but it must remain subordinate to strategy, product truth and delivery.

## Place in the strategy system

- **Strategy** diagnoses a consequential obstacle and chooses a coherent response.
- **Value innovation** may redesign buyer value, cost and the activity system.
- **Positioning** makes the chosen value and difference intelligible, attributable and retrievable.
- **Brand building** develops broader memory structures, assets, experience, availability and equity over time.

A positioning exercise is inappropriate when the actual constraint is poor product value, access, distribution, proof, capability, economics or delivery. Clearer language cannot repair those failures.

## The audience's reference frame

People interpret unfamiliar offers through what they already know. Research the category or alternative they use, the situations that prompt consideration, options retrieved without prompting, associations and decision criteria, rejected language, and non-use. “Ladders” and “slots” are useful metaphors for simplified consideration structures, not literal cognitive models.

The reference frame may be an established category, a familiar alternative, a use situation, a job, a mechanism or a newly proposed category. A new frame must improve comprehension and choice; novelty of vocabulary alone creates no category.

## Positioning architecture

A working internal statement is:

> For **[specific audience in a relevant situation]**, **[brand/offer]** is the **[credible reference frame]** that enables **[important progress]** through **[distinct mechanism or capability]**, unlike **[relevant alternative]**, because **[substantiated reasons to believe]**.

The statement is a decision tool, not necessarily public copy. Its components must be tested separately:

1. **Audience and situation:** meaningful without assuming all buyers think alike.
2. **Reference frame:** immediately understandable and commercially useful.
3. **Promise or association:** important, focused and accurately attributable.
4. **Difference:** perceptible and relevant—not merely verbally unique.
5. **Reasons to believe:** current, representative and proportionate evidence.
6. **Sacrifices:** explicit nonclaims, nonaudiences and activities that will not be pursued.

## Focus and simplicity

Communication overload makes prioritization valuable. A primary association can guide attention and organizational coherence, but simplicity is not permission to conceal risk, qualifications or material limitations. Narrowness is conditional: it must be balanced against sufficient demand, buying situations, future options and inclusion.

## Names, categories and portfolios

Names can aid pronunciation, recognition, search, attribution and category comprehension. Test them across languages, cultures, domains, voice interfaces and legal contexts. Descriptiveness can support comprehension while weakening distinctiveness or protection.

Brand extensions are not inherently harmful. Evaluate audience fit, transfer of associations, distinctive-asset attribution, architecture, cannibalization, investment, operational coherence and future flexibility. The original book's general anti-extension rule is retained only as a risk hypothesis.

## Implementation system

A position becomes credible through aligned product behavior, offer design, price, distribution, experience, proof, identity, vocabulary, content, sales, service, people and incentives. Consistency helps learning and retrieval, but persistence is warranted only while evidence supports the position and the underlying promise remains true.

## Measurement and learning

Measure more than message preference:

- unaided and aided category/brand retrieval in defined situations;
- comprehension and intended versus unintended interpretation;
- association strength, uniqueness and attribution;
- perceived credibility and proof recognition;
- consideration, qualified behavior and downstream outcomes;
- reach across relevant buyer groups and exclusion effects;
- delivery consistency and claim substantiation.

Predefine thresholds and compare alternative positions. Revise when category language, product truth, buyer behavior, alternatives or strategic priorities change.

## Ethical boundaries

Perception does not override reality. Comparative positioning must be fair and substantiated. Do not create advantage through false implication, manufactured fear, stigma, protected identity, health anxiety or omitted material risk. Testimonials, rankings and authority cues require provenance and conflict disclosure. Communities represented by cultural, national or religious positioning require participation rather than imposed simplification.

## Do not infer

- Firstness guarantees leadership or fixed share ratios.
- Every market has one shared mental ladder.
- One word is always the optimal position.
- Repetition makes a claim true or persuasive indefinitely.
- Line extension normally fails in all contexts.
- A memorable position establishes product value, demand or profitable growth.
