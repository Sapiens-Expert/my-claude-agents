# Choice architecture and decisions

## Core premise

The way options are presented, ordered, defaulted, timed, explained, and made easier or harder influences behavior. Choice architecture is unavoidable; ethical use makes its purpose and consequences inspectable while preserving meaningful agency.

## Design levers

- Defaults and active choice.
- Option set, order, grouping, and comparison.
- Salience, framing, social information, and timing.
- Friction, steps, forms, waiting, reminders, and deadlines.
- Feedback, error prevention/recovery, and consequence visibility.
- Translation/mapping from features or probabilities to outcomes people understand.
- Commitments, implementation intentions, environmental cues, and social support.

Every lever can help or exploit. “Friction” may be protective; “simplicity” may hide material consequences; defaults may entrench the designer's interests.

## Ethical gate

Before deployment, answer:

1. Whose outcome is being optimized, and is it consistent with the person's informed interests?
2. Are options, sponsor incentives, data use, material consequences, and uncertainty understandable?
3. Can a person decline, reverse, cancel, or choose differently without disproportionate cost?
4. Does the design exploit scarcity, fatigue, fear, illness, grief, addiction, status, or impaired capacity?
5. Who benefits, who bears burden, and how do effects differ across groups?
6. What would count as harm, regret, misunderstanding, dependency, or coercion?
7. Is a structural, service, policy, or resource change more appropriate than nudging individuals?

## Decision support

Bias labels are prompts for investigation, not diagnoses. Improve consequential decisions by:

- widening the option set and including “do nothing,” delay, and reversible trials;
- separating evidence from narrative and base rates from vivid examples;
- seeking disconfirming evidence and testing assumptions;
- using reference classes/outside views where relevant;
- considering opportunity cost, second-order effects, pre-mortems, and failure recovery;
- creating distance from transient emotion without pretending emotion is irrelevant;
- defining values, constraints, thresholds, and review dates before choosing;
- recording predictions so hindsight does not rewrite the reasoning.

## Habit and behavior models

Useful lenses include cue/routine/reward, trigger/action/reward/investment, and motivation/ability/prompt. They help locate intervention points but are not equivalent theories and do not prove a behavior will become automatic.

Start with the smallest behavior that still matters, attach it to a reliable context, make it feasible, provide immediate informational/reinforcing feedback, and review real-world repetition. Identity can support consistency but must not turn lapses into shame or rigid self-definition.

Commercial habit-forming design needs an additional dependency/welfare review. Variable rewards and escalating investment are not acceptable merely because they improve retention.

## Motivation and scarcity

Autonomy, competence/mastery, purpose/meaning, incentives, identity, social context, ability, energy, and opportunity can interact. Avoid one-factor motivation stories.

Scarcity of time, money, sleep, safety, or attention may narrow focus and reduce planning bandwidth. Do not exploit scarcity through countdowns, complex cancellation, pressure, or additional cognitive load. Reduce demands, create slack, simplify recovery, and address structural constraints.

## Evidence caution

This library mixes foundational scholarship, popular syntheses, anecdotal case books, and commercial habit/product frameworks. Famous demonstrations, named biases, broad “system 1/system 2” explanations, willpower depletion, choice-overload, social-contagion, timing, happiness-prediction, and food-environment claims vary by construct and context. Verify primary evidence, replications, effect size, population, and boundary conditions before consequential claims.
