# Entheogen.expert demand-cluster analysis

Market/evidence research from the "INSTA Strategy" document (2026-08-30), grading demand and content opportunity by evidence type rather than treating all sources as equivalent. **Methodological note carried over from the source, verbatim in spirit: no keyword tools, platform trend dashboards, or Reddit/TikTok comment corpora were accessible while building this. "Search volume unavailable" appears wherever a number would normally sit — nothing here should be cited as verified search demand.** What the analysis does have: peer-reviewed prevalence data, current policy/regulatory movement, and published provider-side market signals, each graded separately below.

## Evidence base by type, not treated as equivalent

| Grade | Type | Strength here | How to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| D. Scientific and research | Peer-reviewed prevalence, phenomenology, adverse-effect surveys | Strongest available — specific numbers, named limitations | Primary content substrate; cite sample sizes out loud |
| C. Media and policy | State programs, FDA pipeline, executive orders | Strong and verifiable | Context and timeliness, never the main claim |
| E. Provider-side promotion | Coach trainings, retreat marketing, integration blogs | Abundant, commercially motivated, low evidential weight | Read as market signal only, never as evidence of demand |
| B. Social conversation | Reddit, TikTok comments, Substack, forums | Directionally informative, not verified here | Treat as hypothesis; verify in Vlad's own comment section |
| A. Search demand | Keyword volume, trend data | Not accessible in this exercise | **Search volume unavailable — do not assert** |

**The single most common error in this niche: treating E as if it were A.** Provider marketing saying demand is rising is not demand data — it's supply data.

## Executive trend summary

**The structural situation.** Legal/quasi-legal access is scaling faster than post-experience support. Oregon's licensed service centers have served roughly 16,000 clients since launch; Colorado has 34 licensed healing centers; New Mexico legalized medical psilocybin through 2025 legislation; COMPASS Pathways hit its primary endpoint in two consecutive Phase 3 trials for treatment-resistant depression with a rolling NDA targeted for final submission Q4 2026; an April 2026 federal executive order accelerated FDA review for psychedelic compounds without changing scheduling. Each of these is an upstream valve — more supervised experiences, more clinical trial participants, more people in Europe traveling to legal jurisdictions — with no matching expansion in the layer that handles what happens afterward. That gap is entheogen.expert's market, and it's widening, not closing.

**The clinical situation — where the content advantage sits.** Post-acute difficulty is not rare and it's measured: a 1,993-person survey of the single most difficult psilocybin experience found 39% rated it among the five most challenging experiences of their life, 11% put themselves or others at physical risk, 2.7% sought medical help, and 24% reported at least one symptom (anxiety, paranoia, depression, or fear) lasting a week or longer. A separate 2,833-person naturalistic survey found 11% with persisting negative effects at two to four weeks and 7% still at two months. Analysis of over 20,000 Global Drug Survey participants found 22.5% of LSD/psilocybin users reported at least one negative post-acute outcome, 6% lasting more than a month. **The number that reframes the whole content territory: 88% of those who experienced adverse effects still regarded them as part of a positive process of growth or integration.** Difficulty and benefit are not opposites here — almost nobody in short-form content says that, and it's exactly the nuance a scientifically literate translator owns.

**The identity and dissociation layer is under-served and under-explained.** A qualitative dissociative-risk study found roughly 23% of participants reported difficulties with self-perception and identity, with depersonalization affecting 16%; the same work found stigma actively blocking access to mental-health services, compounding isolation. Separately, clinical literature warns that psychedelic-related depersonalization-derealization is frequently misdiagnosed as psychosis or as HPPD, which can lead to inappropriate treatment; when the disorder is genuinely present, remission typically requires 50–100 psychotherapy sessions. Read as a content brief: there's a population that's frightened, misattributing symptoms, avoiding clinical care because of stigma, and being misdiagnosed when they do seek help. Referral-literacy content is not a defensive obligation here — it's the highest-value unmet need in the entire territory, and the one thing an evangelist will never produce.

**The evidence is genuinely contested, which is an opportunity rather than a problem.** A 2022 study found mystical-type experience scores positively associated with persisting positive effects three months later. A 2025 double-blind crossover study of 40 healthy participants found the character of the acute experience (pleasant or mixed) was not linked to the direction or intensity of long-term outcomes, and that dread of ego dissolution did not predict negative effects. Those two findings sit uneasily together — that tension is publishable, honest content, and is exactly the register that separates the account from both evangelists and debunkers.

**The provider side is saturating, and that's a positioning gift.** The integration-coaching training market is expanding quickly — multiple 2026/2027 cohorts across competing institutes, at least one accredited self-paced certification marketed as a bestseller with 2,000 students, discounted from $398 to $97. Industry-side content openly frames coach earnings and rising demand as the pitch. The consequence: the market isn't crowded so much as about to be flooded with newly certified coaches using identical vocabulary, and buyers will have no way to tell them apart. **Differentiation will come from what a practitioner refuses to do, not from credentials. Content that demonstrates refusal is now the scarce asset.**

## Top demand clusters (12, each carrying the same evidence-graded fields)

**Cluster 1 — the insight-action gap.** Evidence: D and E. Integration is defined in the literature as the process by which an experience translates into daily-life change, which presupposes the translation often fails; commentary in the field notes it's common to settle back into old patterns and for neuroplasticity spikes to return to baseline relatively quickly. User language: "nothing changed," "it didn't stick," "I understood everything and did nothing." Underlying need: relief from self-blame plus behavioral structure. Segment: the Stalled, the Faded. Commercial relevance: **highest of any cluster.** Platform risk: very low, no substance content required. Content opportunity: own the phrase "insight is not yet change" as a repeatable series. Hook direction: "Three weeks later, nothing has changed. That's the most common outcome and almost nobody says so." Confidence: **High** — mechanism supported by research framing, demand inferred from the entire existence of the integration category.

**Cluster 2 — the early post-experience window.** Evidence: D and E. Prevalence data shows the two-to-four-week window is when persisting negative effects are measured and most often resolve; provider-side writing consistently flags this period for sleep disturbance and interpersonal change. User language: "I got back last week, how long does this last, should I be making decisions right now." Underlying need: to slow down, and structure that doesn't depend on the feeling lasting. Segment: the Returner. Commercial relevance: medium — high DM volume, poor decision quality, convert late not now. Platform risk: low. Content opportunity: a fixed 14-day framework, entirely behavioral, no substance content. Hook: "You want to change everything this week. Wait fourteen days. Here's why that's not caution." Confidence: **High.**

**Cluster 3 — difficult experiences and referral literacy.** Evidence: D, strong (see prevalence figures above — 39% top-five-hardest, 11% physical risk, 2.7% medical help, 24% symptom lasting a week+, 6.7% in a separate analysis reporting thoughts or attempts of harming self or others). User language: "something is wrong with me, is this permanent, I don't know who to ask." Underlying need: accurate triage between what settles and what needs licensed care. Segment: the Shaken, part of the Wrong Room. Commercial relevance: zero by design — and still the highest-value content in the system, because handled correctly it points away from use entirely. Platform risk: medium, requires careful framing. Content opportunity: a public, repeated statement of what's outside coaching, delivered without a CTA. Hook: "One in four people report something lasting more than a week. Here's what that means and what it does not mean." Confidence: **High.**

**Cluster 4 — DPDR, derealization, and misdiagnosis risk.** Evidence: D, strong and specific (16% depersonalization, 23% self-perception/identity difficulty in the dissociation-focused study; clinical literature on misdiagnosis as psychosis or HPPD; remission requiring 50–100 sessions when the disorder is genuinely present; HPPD prevalence contested, one clinician-facing source citing up to 1-in-25 lifetime users — cite with its uncertainty attached). User language: "everything looks slightly wrong, I don't feel real, is this permanent, never-ending trip." Underlying need: to be believed, assessed correctly, and not made worse by a wrong label. Segment: the Shaken, the Wrong Room. Commercial relevance: zero — absolute no-sell zone. Platform risk: high if handled loosely, low if handled as pure referral education. Content opportunity: the most under-served topic in the territory — explaining that misdiagnosis is a documented risk, and how to describe symptoms to a clinician, is a service almost nobody provides. Hook: "If the world still looks slightly wrong weeks later, this is not a coaching question. It is also more commonly misdiagnosed than you'd expect." Confidence: **High on the clinical facts. Medium on audience size**, since search volume is unavailable.

**Cluster 5 — ontological shock and identity confusion.** Evidence: D and B. Identity/self-perception difficulty documented at ~23% in the dissociation sample; social-conversation evidence is widespread in published essays but not quantified here. User language: "I don't know what's real, was that true or was it my brain." Underlying need: a way to hold significance without requiring metaphysical certainty. Segment: the Destabilized. Commercial relevance: high — intellectually engaged, values rigor, converts on credibility. Platform risk: low. Content opportunity: separating "meaningful" from "accurate" as two distinct questions — a philosophical position almost nobody in the space holds cleanly, since evangelists collapse them and debunkers dismiss both. Hook: "It meant something. That doesn't make it true. You don't have to resolve that this month." Confidence: **Medium to high.**

**Cluster 6 — emotional flatness and detachment.** Evidence: D, moderate. Persisting negative effects at 2–4 weeks and 2 months include affective symptoms in the survey literature; overlaps with depersonalization but must be distinguished, not merged. User language: "I feel nothing, flat, numb, disconnected, I cannot access emotion anymore." Underlying need: to know whether this is a normal post-peak trough, a depressive process, or dissociation. Segment: the Shaken, the Stalled. Commercial relevance: low to medium, only after clinical possibilities are named. Platform risk: medium — must not be framed as a coaching-solvable symptom. Content opportunity: distinguishing a contrast effect after an intense state from something requiring assessment. Hook: "Flatness after an intense experience has more than one explanation, and only some of them are mine to work with." Confidence: **Medium.**

**Cluster 7 — relationship friction after an experience.** Evidence: E and D-adjacent, not well quantified directly. User language: "my partner thinks I've lost it, nobody around me understands, I came back different." Underlying need: language that works with a skeptical person who wasn't there. Segment: the Destabilized, the Returner. Commercial relevance: medium to high — often the pain that finally triggers a purchase, since it's the one with an external cost. Platform risk: very low, fully platform-safe. Content opportunity: scripts for talking to a partner — highly saveable, highly shareable, and shares here go to a specific named person, the strongest algorithmic signal available. Hook: "Your partner is not dismissing you. They're watching you use a vocabulary they have no access to." Confidence: **Medium** — high content value, weaker prevalence evidence.

**Cluster 8 — no-visions disappointment.** Evidence: D, and unusually good — the same 2025 crossover study found the character of the acute experience wasn't linked to long-term outcome direction or intensity, and dread of ego dissolution didn't predict negative effects; a 2022 study found mystical-type experience intensity associated with persisting positive effects. **The field does not agree with itself.** User language: "I got nothing, it didn't work for me, everyone else had visions." Underlying need: reassurance that absence of spectacle doesn't mean absence of usable material, delivered honestly rather than as consolation. Segment: the Stalled, the Re-doser. Commercial relevance: high — the doorway to the escalation conversation. Platform risk: very low. Content opportunity: present both findings, name the disagreement, refuse to resolve it — the strongest single piece of evidence-versus-story content available. Hook: "One study says intensity predicts the outcome. Another says it doesn't. Here's what that actually means for you." Confidence: **High — both studies verified.**

**Cluster 9 — re-dosing and escalation as substitute for integration.** Evidence: B and E, inferred — not directly quantified in the sources reviewed, supported indirectly by field observation that people chase repeat revelation while confusing access to insight with the work of change. User language: "the last one wasn't enough, thinking about a bigger one, I keep going back." Underlying need: to have the dose-versus-structure question named without shame. Segment: the Re-doser. Commercial relevance: **highest willingness to pay in the audience** — budget already allocated to this category. Platform risk: **medium to high** — this is the cluster most likely to drift into dose talk. Rule: never mention amount, never mention direction of change, discuss only the behavioral question. Content opportunity: reallocating one experience budget into a structured container, argued as arithmetic rather than a pitch. Hook: "Before you book another one, answer one question. What did you do differently after the last one?" Confidence: **Medium** — strong commercial logic, weaker direct demand evidence; verify in Vlad's own comments before scaling it.

**Cluster 10 — coaching versus therapy versus clinical care.** Evidence: D and E. Research documents stigma blocking access to mental-health services; provider-side material openly (and correctly) frames integration coaching as goal-oriented, non-medical support, but this is being said by a rapidly growing number of newly certified practitioners. User language: "do I need a therapist or a coach, will they judge me if I say what I took, is coaching enough." Underlying need: a map of which professional does what, and permission to be honest with a clinician. Segment: all of them — qualifies inbound before it reaches the DM. Commercial relevance: high, indirectly — reduces wrong-fit inquiries, increases right-fit ones. Platform risk: low. Content opportunity: the buyer-protection angle — how to assess a practitioner, what to ask before paying, what a competent one refers out. Hook: "Ask any integration coach this before you pay them: what do you refer out? If they can't answer quickly, keep looking." Confidence: **High.**

**Cluster 11 — preparation without fantasy.** Evidence: D. Risk factors identified in the survey literature include estimated dose, duration, difficulty, and absence of physical comfort/social support; prior experience does not appear protective against difficult experiences; contraindication knowledge gaps are explicitly flagged for people with psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, and trauma history. User language: "how do I prepare, what should I expect, am I ready, what if it goes badly." Underlying need: honest personal risk assessment rather than reassurance. Segment: the Researcher. Commercial relevance: medium, delayed — free asset only. Platform risk: **highest cluster on the account.** Preparation content sits closest to instruction — categories and questions only, never advice, never logistics, never venues. Content opportunity: prior experience not being protective is counterintuitive, evidenced, and safe to say. Hook: "Having done it before does not protect you. The research is fairly clear on that, and it surprises people." Confidence: **High on evidence. Medium on commercial value.**

**Cluster 12 — evidence versus story.** Evidence: D, abundant, but sample sizes small and designs vary (the 2025 crossover study: 40 participants; the 2022 mystical-experience study: 28 volunteers; the large prevalence surveys are self-selected online samples with a heavy male skew). User language: "studies show, science proves, they did a study on this." Underlying need: to stop being lied to by both promoters and dismissers. Segment: all — the reach layer for the enthusiast audience. Commercial relevance: low directly, very high for credibility. Platform risk: very low. Content opportunity: study literacy as a recurring format — name the sample size out loud, every time. Hook: "That study had twenty-eight people in it. It's still interesting. It's not what the caption said it proved." Confidence: **High.**

## Search-intent map (search volume unavailable for every row — intent classification is analytical, based on how phrasing clusters in published material and clinical presentations, not measured query data)

| Intent type | What the person is doing | Content format that matches | Commercial value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symptom checking | Trying to find out if they're okay | Referral literacy, mechanism explainer | None — serve, do not sell |
| Explanation seeking | Has a phenomenon, wants a name for it | Definition plus mechanism, 40 seconds | Medium |
| Failure diagnosis | Something didn't work, wants to know why | Recognition open, core series | **Highest** |
| Decision support | Choosing between options | Comparison, question set | High |
| Preparation research | Pre-event information gathering | Question set, contraindication categories | Medium, delayed |
| Provider evaluation | Looking to hire | Buyer-protection content, pinned profile assets | **Highest per unit of effort** |
| Reassurance seeking | Distressed, wants to not be alone | Comment engagement, not video | None — refer |
| Evidence checking | Skeptical, verifying a claim | Study-literacy format | Low direct, high credibility |

**The two rows to build the content system around: failure diagnosis and provider evaluation.** Failure diagnosis has volume and matches the primary segment (the Stalled). Provider evaluation has intent and converts (the Buyer). Everything else is trust infrastructure.

## Hidden-demand map (needs that exist but never surface as an obvious query)

- **The stagnation shame.** Nobody searches "I'm ashamed a profound experience didn't change me" — they search around it. The unspoken question is whether they're defective in a way others aren't. Content naming the shame directly, before offering the mechanism, outperforms content that leads with the mechanism.
- **The suspicion they're avoiding something.** The Re-doser rarely asks "am I using this to avoid my life" — they ask about doses, settings, venues. The real question is underneath and they won't type it. A video that asks it on their behalf, without judgment, converts unusually well because it says the thing they couldn't.
- **Fear of the clinical system.** Stigma actively hinders access to mental-health services (documented), so the demand doesn't appear as "find me a psychiatrist" — it appears as searching for anyone who is not a psychiatrist. This is the most dangerous hidden demand in the territory, because it routes distressed people toward coaches by design. Content addressing the fear itself, and how to talk to a clinician without being penalized, meets a need nobody else is meeting.
- **Wanting permission to stop.** Some people want to hear they can stop doing this and that stopping isn't failure. No one in a market built on selling experiences will ever say it. Saying it is both honest and differentiating.
- **Wanting to be told it's not too late.** The Faded segment has no query for this — it surfaces as nostalgia. The demand is for permission, not information.
- **Wanting to assess the practitioner without appearing distrustful.** Buyers can't easily ask "how do I know you're not a fraud." Publishing the criteria yourself removes the need to ask and makes Vlad the source of the standard.

## Topics to avoid

**Absolute — no version of these works, regardless of framing:** dosing, quantities, ranges, or comparisons of amount; sourcing, vendors, or where to obtain anything; preparation of any material; combinations, stacking, or interactions; specific retreat/venue names, including critical mentions (a classifier doesn't distinguish endorsement from criticism); legal advice about any jurisdiction; anything readable as encouragement to use.

**Avoid because they misposition, not because they're unsafe:** personal-experience storytelling as a primary format (converts enthusiasts, dilutes the translator role — transformation narratives, his own or clients', even composites, read as testimony); culture-war content about stigma, prohibition, or drug-policy commentary (reach without qualification, signals the wrong category to the exact audience he wants); mystical/ceremonial vocabulary, even when quoting an audience member back to themselves; comparisons to therapy that position coaching as superior — it isn't, and claiming otherwise is exactly the overreach the flooded coach market is about to be judged for.

**Avoid because the evidence doesn't support the strength of the claim:** any specific neuroscience mechanism presented as settled (the literature is thinner than the content ecosystem implies); any claim that integration improves outcomes (plausible, widely believed, not demonstrated at the level a confident claim would require — say what integration is *for*, not what it guarantees); microdosing efficacy claims in either direction; HPPD prevalence stated as fact (the 1-in-25 figure exists but is contested and should always carry its uncertainty).

**Avoid because the commercial temptation is strongest exactly where the ethics are tightest:** any content addressing acute distress that carries a CTA, a link, or a soft invitation. Clusters 3 and 4 are permanently CTA-free. If that rule ever bends, the whole positioning collapses, because the entire differentiation rests on demonstrable refusal.

## Best 10 topics to create first (sequenced — first five establish the position, next five test unverified demand)

**Establish (weeks 1–2):** (1) "Three weeks later, nothing has changed" — Cluster 1, the anchor, everything else supports it. (2) "What I do not do" — not a provider, not a clinician, no recommendations, refers out; pin permanently, does the qualification work for every subsequent video. (3) "When this is not a coaching problem" — Clusters 3 and 4, no CTA, names symptom categories, points to licensed local care, stops. (4) "Two studies, opposite conclusions" — the 2022 vs. 2025 result, establishes the translator role in one video. (5) "Before you book another one" — Cluster 9, highest-value segment, needs to be in the early set so the comment section starts producing data on it immediately.

**Test (weeks 3–4):** (1) "One in four report something lasting a week or more" — tests whether prevalence framing produces recognition or anxiety in this specific audience; watch comment quality closely. (2) "Your partner is not dismissing you" — tests the relationship-friction cluster, weakest evidence, strongest share mechanics. (3) "I got nothing. No visions." — tests the no-visions cluster with the 2025 finding attached. (4) "Ask any coach what they refer out" — tests buyer-evaluation intent; low reach expected, high DM quality if it lands. (5) "It has been two years and I still tell the story" — tests the Faded segment, the largest untested pool in the audience map.

**What to measure across these ten.** Not views. Qualified comments per video, saves per thousand views, and whether the DMs that follow are from segment 5, 8, or 10. Ten videos is enough to tell which of the inferred (Grade I) phrases above are real demand and which were a plausible-sounding hypothesis — promote winners into named series, drop the rest without sentiment.

**One honest caveat on the whole document.** The clinical and policy layers are solid and cited. The audience-language layer is inference from segment psychology, and it's the layer the business depends on. Treat the first ten videos as the actual research instrument — the comment section will produce better demand data in three weeks than any keyword tool would give today.
