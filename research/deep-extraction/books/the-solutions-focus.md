# The Solutions Focus

## Bibliographic record

- Authors: Paul Z. Jackson and Mark McKergow.
- Edition/year/language: first edition, 2002, English.
- Publisher/identifier: Nicholas Brealey Publishing; ISBN 1-85788-270-9.
- Source paths and exact duplicates: one archived PDF; SHA-256 `afc379a37337b3e238ec2226fd891c5c853f4c752cae8b92a87823ce6e100c8f`.
- File completeness/extraction quality: 223 pages; complete front matter, 14 chapters, references, bibliography, index, and author biographies; good extraction; no OCR.
- Authority and intended audience: organizational-practice adaptation of solution-focused brief therapy for managers, coaches, facilitators, teams, and consultants. It contains an early research overview but is primarily a practitioner book with cases rather than a current independent systematic review.

## Bottom line

- Central thesis: in complex human interaction, progress often comes faster from defining the desired future, finding when elements already occur, using available resources, and taking small fitted actions than from building a complete causal account of the problem.
- Strongest contribution: the SIMPLE principles plus a compact toolkit—platform, future perfect, counters, scales, affirmations, and small actions—organized around observation and local fit.
- Main limitation: the book sometimes generalizes from brief-therapy and organizational cases toward broad effectiveness claims; its 2002 evidence review, complexity analogies, and anti-explanation rhetoric do not justify abandoning diagnosis or root-cause analysis where causal understanding protects safety or reliability.
- Safe VladOS use: apply as a hypothesis-generating and action-learning method for non-acute human/organizational change, with an explicit gate for risk, clinical needs, structural causes, accountability, and recurring system failure.

## Argument map

The authors argue that conventional problem solving is highly effective in many technical and medical domains but can become counterproductive in complex human interaction. Explanations may invite blame, harden labels, privilege experts, or absorb attention without creating action. Because no interpersonal problem occurs identically all the time, exceptions reveal already-working elements. Describing a concrete preferred future, locating counters and resources, scaling the present position, affirming contributions, and trying small actions creates an iterative route forward.

Systems and complexity ideas support the claim that behavior emerges through interaction and that small differences may propagate. Language helps construct what participants notice and attempt, but the authors caution that words are personal and are not the world itself. Each case therefore requires local observation rather than one preselected theory. Cases across coaching, teams, and organizations illustrate the method; the historical chapter connects it to solution-focused brief therapy and reports early outcome literature.

## Frameworks and processes

### SIMPLE principles

- **S — Solutions, not problems:** focus on desired change and useful difference.
- **I — Inbetween:** observe interaction patterns rather than assigning internal defect or blame.
- **M — Make use of what is there:** identify resources, cooperation, and exceptions.
- **P — Possibilities:** mine past, present, and future for workable options.
- **L — Language:** use concrete, simple, client-owned descriptions and scales.
- **E — Every case is different:** fit action to this context and learn from response.

### Solution-building loop

1. Establish the platform, stakeholders, legitimate customer for change, constraints and safety.
2. Describe the future perfect in observable, interactional terms.
3. Find counters: when, where, with whom, or in what form desired elements already occur.
4. Map resources and affirm specific contributions.
5. Use a locally meaningful scale to locate progress and useful differences.
6. Choose the smallest ethical action likely to produce information or movement.
7. Observe what changes, do more of what works, stop/alter what does not, and repeat.

### VladOS causal-analysis gate

Use solution-building without exhaustive problem analysis only when doing so does not hide acute risk, safeguarding, abuse, medical/psychological diagnosis, legal duty, engineering failure, security, financial control, structural discrimination, or repeated operational causes. In those cases, pair or replace it with appropriate root-cause investigation and expertise.

## Examples and cases

Cases cover safety-regulator relationships, organizational change, coaching, teams, conflict, rehabilitation and other interactions. They show how questions and small shifts could be used and provide process hypotheses. They are retrospective, selected, and largely uncontrolled; reported improvement does not isolate the method or establish general effect size.

## Contradictions and complements

- Complements [Motivational Interviewing](motivational-interviewing-4e.md) through collaboration, client language, resources, autonomy, evocation and small planning steps.
- MI attends more explicitly to ambivalence, directional ethics, fidelity and evidence; Solutions Focus more aggressively redirects attention away from problem explanation.
- Complements [Co-Active Coaching](co-active-coaching-4e.md) on curiosity, resourcefulness, future possibility, action and learning, while offering more observable tools and less intuitive interpretation.
- Conflicts with any VladOS use that substitutes future/exception talk for medical, psychological, safety, tax/legal or system diagnosis.
- Corrects deficit-heavy “resistance” narratives but can itself become invalidating when a person needs acknowledgment, grief, accountability or causal repair.

## Applications

- Knowledge: solution-building as one mode within a broader diagnostic repertoire.
- Skills: future-perfect description, exceptions/counters, scaling, resource affirmation and small experiments.
- Frameworks: add a causal-analysis gate when later synthesizing coaching models.
- Projects/offers: focus coaching on observable progress without promising universal speed or simplicity.
- SOPs/templates: include “what is already working?”, “why not lower?”, and smallest-test prompts after cross-book comparison.
- Content/research questions: explain solution-focused versus solution-forced practice and when root-cause analysis remains necessary.

No canonical knowledge or framework is changed from this source alone.

## Limitations and do-not-infer

- Publication/edition age: 2002; evidence, terminology, professional ethics, systems practice and SFBT reviews require updating.
- Population/context limits: therapy evidence does not automatically transfer to organizations, and organizational cases do not establish clinical outcomes.
- Methodological limits: early review and selected cases; few quantitative details; complexity and linguistic philosophy function partly as analogy.
- Commercial/ideological conflicts: authors sell solution-focused consulting/training and advocate their named SIMPLE model.
- Safety/scope issues: reduced problem analysis can miss danger, abuse, diagnosis, structural causes, legal obligations and recurring technical failure.
- Do not infer that causes never matter, all problems contain accessible exceptions, small steps always suffice, optimism is harmless, experts are unnecessary, or the method is proven for every context.
