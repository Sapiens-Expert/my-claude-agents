# Building Brand Communities

## Bibliographic record

- Carrie Melissa Jones and Charles H. Vogl, *Building Brand Communities*.
- Berrett-Koehler Publishers, first edition, 2020. Hardcover ISBN 978-1-5230-8661-0; PDF ebook ISBN 978-1-5230-8662-7.
- Coverage: preface, introduction, all 11 chapters, both appendices, glossary and notes.
- Authority: practitioner synthesis drawing on community scholarship, interviews and cases; useful for design, not proof that a community causes a business outcome.

## Bottom line

A community is a group whose members share mutual concern for one another. A brand community must serve members and may also serve a clearly chosen organizational goal. Followers, customers, an audience, a mailing list or an engagement feed are not automatically communities. The decisive tests are voluntary choice, human connection, progress toward a shared purpose and evidence of mutual concern.

## Community reality test

Do not use the word “community” until there is evidence for all of the following:

1. People can freely choose whether and how to participate.
2. Members form relationships with one another, not only with the brand.
3. Participation helps people progress toward a shared purpose.
4. Members demonstrate mutual concern, not merely common consumption.
5. A meaningful boundary distinguishes visitors, participants, members and stewards.
6. Members can describe value in their own terms.

If the evidence shows one-way publishing, reach or transactions, call it an audience, channel, customer base, program or network.

## Dual-purpose compact

Document two linked but non-identical purposes:

- **Member purpose:** why people gather and what progress, belonging or support they seek.
- **Organizational goal:** the single initial job the investment is expected to support.

The book identifies seven common organizational goals: innovation; talent recruitment and retention; customer retention; marketing; customer service; advancing a movement; and becoming a forum/destination for a specific community. Member welfare constrains organizational optimization. If the brand goal undermines the member purpose, the community claim fails.

## Participation conditions

The book's three conditions form a useful design gate:

- **Choice:** participation and membership are voluntary.
- **Connection:** people can form genuine relationships.
- **Progress:** activity advances a shared purpose.

These are necessary design conditions, not outcome proof. Add consent, accessibility, safety and the ability to leave.

## Founding and maturity

Begin by listening to potential members and selecting founding members who embody the intended values and need the shared purpose. They constitute the initial community; the brand cannot predefine every norm without them.

| Stage | Observable condition | Leadership priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inception | Founders constitute the group | Clarify need, purpose, values and invitations |
| Establishment | Return and self-organization emerge | Support norms, roles and member initiative |
| Critical mass | Members uphold norms and bring resources | Distribute authority; balance growth and depth |
| Segmentation | Subcommunities form around narrower needs | Preserve umbrella values while allowing local norms |

The book offers a 50–60% returning-participant rate as one establishment benchmark and notes that a core minority may take leadership at critical mass. Treat both as heuristics, not universal targets. Quiet participants may still receive value; visible posting is not the sole proof of membership.

## Boundaries, belonging and inclusion

Boundaries can create safety, identity and commitment, but also exclusion and status concentration. Specify entry, participation, moderation, role and appeal rules. Audit whether inner rings reproduce privilege or suppress dissent. Belonging is first-person evidence: ask members whether they feel recognized, accepted, safe and able to contribute; do not infer belonging from attendance or clicks.

## Governance and role design

Community leaders facilitate purpose, relationships and norms; managers provide logistics, systems and continuity. In practice, add:

- a published code of conduct and enforcement ladder;
- safeguarding, crisis and referral protocols appropriate to the topic;
- transparent role selection, training, rotation and removal;
- conflict-of-interest and commercial-disclosure rules;
- member feedback, appeal and incident-reporting paths;
- succession and continuity beyond a charismatic founder;
- compensation or workload limits for material labor.

Distributed leadership does not remove organizational accountability.

## Shared experiences and space

Strong shared experiences reinforce values, are recognizable to insiders, have temporal boundaries and occur in a shared physical or digital space. Design recurring small-group “campfire” experiences where people can encounter one another, not only consume a presentation. Participation intensity and disclosure must remain optional. Provide accessible formats, behavioral expectations and trained facilitation.

## Content strategy

Community content should help members pursue the purpose, understand norms, learn from one another and initiate useful exchange. A brand content calendar alone is audience marketing. Prefer prompts, member knowledge, rituals, orientation resources, case reflection and searchable collective memory. Obtain consent for reuse and never convert vulnerable disclosures into marketing assets by default.

## Motivation without manipulation

Encourage contribution by reducing friction, making purpose and roles clear, recognizing meaningful work and showing progress. Avoid forced posting, public shame, artificial scarcity, status games and rewards that crowd out intrinsic motivation. Never manufacture interpersonal obligation to meet commercial targets.

## Measurement stack

Use three evidence layers:

| Layer | Example questions |
| --- | --- |
| Member experience | Do people report belonging, useful relationships, safety and progress? |
| Community health | Do people return, help one another, initiate activity, resolve conflict and share leadership? |
| Organizational outcome | Is the chosen goal improving, with a plausible contribution pathway and acceptable cost? |

Segment results by role, tenure and relevant access factors. Pair quantitative trends with interviews and incident data. Engagement is an input signal, not the objective. Attribute cautiously: active communities may result from strong products or pre-existing affinity rather than cause them.

## Safety and ethical risk register

- Commercializing loneliness or vulnerability can create dependency.
- Brand control can masquerade as member ownership.
- Inner rings can become exclusionary, discriminatory or abusive.
- Volunteer work, stories and relationships can be extracted for corporate value.
- Movement, health, faith and psychedelic contexts carry heightened power and misinformation risks.
- Growth can outrun moderation, safeguarding and new-member integration.
- Public activity metrics can penalize quiet or accessibility-constrained members.
- Shared values can harden into conformity; preserve dissent and appeal.

## VladOS applications

- **LinkedIn:** call followers and newsletter readers an audience unless member-to-member concern and shared governance emerge. Use content to invite useful peer exchange, not to fake community signals.
- **Executive coaching:** alumni circles need explicit confidentiality, facilitation and referral boundaries; participation should never be required to preserve access to core services.
- **Entheogen Expert/integration groups:** require screening, scope limits, facilitator competence, crisis escalation, privacy, nonclinical disclaimers and prohibition of sourcing or unsafe-use advice.
- **Sapiens Healthspan:** organize around safe progress and peer support while separating education from medical care and protecting health data.
- **Brand architecture:** keep each community's member purpose and risk profile distinct; do not merge audiences merely for cross-selling efficiency.
