# The Story Grid — Shawn Coyne

## Bibliographic record

- *The Story Grid: What Good Editors Know*; first Black Irish Entertainment edition, April 2015.
- ISBN 978-1-936891-35-1; ebook ISBN 978-1-936891-36-8.
- One 360-page English PDF; SHA-256 `8ac5c347c21a30d025ab086a090c2cab8b182b5f6889764c5db60b8dff044bf5`; about 92,388 extracted words; no OCR required.
- Complete coverage: preface, introduction, eight parts, all 70 chapters, epilogue, acknowledgments and the full *Silence of the Lambs* worked analysis.
- Authority: a veteran commercial editor's proprietary synthesis of narrative theory and publishing experience. Strong as a diagnostic workflow; its universal, numerical and market-success claims are practitioner hypotheses rather than established laws.

## Bottom line

The Story Grid turns developmental editing into a visible macro-to-micro inspection. It asks whether a story makes a coherent promise, delivers the transformations and moments its audience expects, and creates meaningful change at every structural level. Its lasting value is disciplined diagnosis before revision: map what is actually on the page, identify connected problems globally and only then design repairs. VladOS should adopt this diagnostic separation and its compact planning artifacts without treating its taxonomy, ratios or scene formula as compulsory for every narrative.

## Core distinction: writer mode and editor mode

Coyne separates generation from evaluation. Writer mode needs enough orientation to keep producing; editor mode needs distance, evidence and systematic attention. Premature line-by-line repair can optimize one scene while damaging dependencies elsewhere.

### Governed mode-switch protocol

1. In **planning mode**, define only the minimum global promise and direction necessary to start.
2. In **writer mode**, complete a meaningful draft without continually reopening settled passages.
3. Create distance through time or a format change.
4. In **editor mode**, inventory what is actually present—not what was intended.
5. Diagnose the whole system before proposing local repairs.
6. Write a revision plan ordered by structural dependency.
7. Return to writer mode to execute the plan.
8. Re-run the relevant diagnostics and obtain independent reader evidence.

This is a default workflow, not a ban on all iterative drafting. Short pieces and discovery writing may benefit from smaller cycles.

## Tool 1 — audience-promise and genre contract

Coyne defines genre primarily as a promise about the experience. His five-leaf model asks for choices about:

1. **Time:** short, medium or long form.
2. **Reality:** factual, realistic, absurd or fantastic relationship to the world.
3. **Style:** drama, comedy, documentary, literary and other modes of experience.
4. **Structure:** broadly causal/closed, minimalist/open or deliberately nontraditional plotting.
5. **Content:** the external value pursued consciously and the internal value or transformation needed.

Translate this into a governed contract:

| Question | Artifact |
| --- | --- |
| What experience is being promised, to whom and in what context? | Audience-promise sentence |
| Which recognizable conventions help the audience orient? | Convention inventory with source examples |
| Which moments are necessary to fulfil this specific promise? | Expected-moment checklist |
| Which convention will be refreshed, combined or challenged? | Innovation hypothesis |
| What confusion or harm could arise if a convention is omitted or reproduced uncritically? | Expectation and representation risk |

“Obligatory” means necessary to the chosen promise, not universally mandatory. Validate the inventory through multiple works, audience research and cultural context rather than copying one genre list.

## Tool 2 — one-page global story brief

The Foolscap Global Story Grid compresses a draft or idea into six questions:

1. What is the primary genre or audience promise?
2. Which conventions and expected moments fulfil it?
3. What point of view governs access to information and experience?
4. What does the protagonist consciously want, and what do they need or avoid internally?
5. What controlling idea links outcome to the decisive human action?
6. What are the Beginning Hook, Middle Build and Ending Payoff?

For VladOS, add four governance fields:

- evidence/reality status: factual, composite, fictionalized or invented;
- intended reader change: understanding, feeling, decision or action;
- representation and consent risks;
- claims or outcomes that must not be dramatized beyond the evidence.

The brief should expose contradictions, not force false neatness. If it cannot be completed after a draft, that is a diagnostic signal rather than automatic proof that the work fails.

## Tool 3 — five-movement change diagnostic

Coyne's “five commandments” describe a causal movement:

1. **Inciting incident:** an event disrupts the current balance.
2. **Progressive complications:** obstacles escalate and make prior strategies insufficient.
3. **Crisis:** the character confronts a consequential choice, often between bad alternatives or incompatible goods.
4. **Climax:** the character acts on the choice.
5. **Resolution:** consequences establish the new state.

Apply the diagnostic at the largest useful unit first, then to acts, sequences or scenes only where the story feels static or incoherent. Ask:

- What changed, for whom, and why does it matter?
- Did the complication genuinely narrow options or merely add activity?
- Is the crisis earned by prior causality?
- Does the climax express agency, value or revelation rather than coincidence alone?
- Does the resolution reveal consequences proportionate to the promise?

Do not require all five movements to be explicit in every conversational beat. Fragmentary, lyrical, episodic, reflective and anti-plot forms may organize reader experience differently.

## Tool 4 — scene-change and turning-point audit

A scene earns its place when it produces a meaningful change in knowledge, relationship, power, safety, commitment, identity or another story value.

| Field | Diagnostic |
| --- | --- |
| Scene number and length | Where is narrative attention being allocated? |
| Story event | What happens, expressed in one observable sentence? |
| Value at opening and close | What consequential condition changes? |
| Direction | Positive-to-negative, negative-to-positive, better-to-best, worse-to-worst, or a more complex qualified move? |
| Turning point | Which action or revelation makes the change irreversible within the scene? |
| Function | Which global promise, arc, convention or setup/payoff does it serve? |

If no event or change can be named, determine whether the material is exposition that should be integrated, a reflective passage serving another function, or genuinely removable. Avoid manufacturing artificial reversals merely to populate the table.

## Tool 5 — continuity and distribution audit

Coyne's spreadsheet adds point of view, period/time, duration, location, onstage characters and referenced offstage characters. These fields uncover contradictions and repetitive distribution.

VladOS extends this to:

- whose knowledge and interiority are available;
- elapsed time and causal feasibility;
- setting changes and sensory specificity;
- character presence, absence and mention;
- distribution of agency, speech, expertise and consequence;
- setup/payoff dependencies;
- consent, confidentiality and composite-character status in nonfiction.

Use quantitative patterns as questions. A long scene, repeated viewpoint or uneven speaking distribution may be intentional; the data prompts inspection rather than an automatic edit.

## Macro-to-micro editorial workflow

1. Read once for uninterrupted reader experience and record only the closing impression.
2. Complete the one-page global story brief.
3. Inventory every scene and its word count.
4. Reduce each scene to one observable event.
5. Record value movement and the action/revelation turning point.
6. Track continuity and representation fields relevant to the project.
7. Compare scene-level evidence with genre promises and global arcs.
8. List structural problems without revising them yet.
9. Identify root problems whose repair will resolve multiple local symptoms.
10. Prioritize global promise and causality, then sequence/scene function, then line craft.
11. Draft a revision map with affected dependencies and validation criteria.
12. Revise, reread as an audience member and test with appropriate independent readers.

## Heuristics, not rules

- **25/50/25:** beginning, middle and ending proportions can help estimate pacing, but should not be treated as a universal distribution.
- **About 2,000 words per scene/chapter:** a commercial-page-turner heuristic, not a general optimum.
- **Around 50–70 scenes for a novel:** a planning convenience tied to assumed length and scene size.
- **Every scene must turn:** useful against inert material, but “change” can be subtle, delayed or cumulative.
- **Action/revelation variety:** repetition can dull surprise, but pattern and recurrence can also create meaning.
- **Genre experts as validators:** knowledgeable readers are valuable, but broad audiences, marginalized readers and cross-cultural contexts may perceive promises differently.

## Evidence and conceptual corrections

- The framework draws on Coyne's long editorial career, narrative theorists and close analysis. It is practice-based, not supported here by controlled comparative research.
- Bestseller and sales anecdotes cannot isolate the framework's causal contribution; selection, author skill, distribution, timing and marketing confound the outcome.
- *The Silence of the Lambs* is a rich demonstration but a single-case retrospective fit. The analysis risks confirmation bias and should be compared with failures, counterexamples and other forms.
- Coyne maps a version of the Kübler-Ross grief stages onto narrative pacing. The original model was not a universal ordered law of grieving, and its transplantation does not validate 25/50/25 story structure. VladOS may use shock–resistance–choice–integration as an optional dramatic analogy only, never as psychological education, coaching diagnosis or a model of how real people must change.
- “Internal need” should not authorize an editor to diagnose a real author's or subject's psychology. Keep analysis inside the represented text.
- Audience satisfaction is not the only value. Ethical representation, originality, truth, accessibility and artistic intent can justify challenging expectation.

## Applications beyond fiction

| Context | Safe adaptation |
| --- | --- |

- **Knowledge nodes:** developmental editing; genre as audience expectation; narrative causality; external/internal desire; scene and sequence structure; point of view; narrative values; continuity; revision dependencies.
- **Reusable skills:** audience-promise contract; convention/expected-moment audit; one-page global story brief; five-movement change diagnostic; scene-change spreadsheet; continuity/distribution audit; macro-to-micro revision map.
- **Primary applications:** book development, narrative nonfiction, case studies, brand storytelling, video/podcast scripting, course narratives, LinkedIn posts and editorial QA.
- **Do not encode as universal skills:** fixed scene lengths/counts, 25/50/25 as law, mandatory visible turns at every beat, the Kübler-Ross story curve or the claim that structural compliance predicts sales.
