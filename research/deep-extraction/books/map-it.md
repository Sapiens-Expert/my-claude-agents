# Map It

## Bibliographic record

- Cathy Moore, *Map It: The Hands-On Guide to Strategic Training Design*.
- Montesa Press, 2017. ISBN 978-0-9991745-0-0.
- Coverage: introduction, all 14 chapters, conclusion, four appendices and bibliography.
- Authority: detailed professional manual for action mapping and workplace performance improvement, grounded in practitioner experience and selected training/learning research. It is not a systematic evidence review.
- Scope: designed for adults performing current workplace tasks in business, government and NGOs, not as a general model for academic education.
- Source caveat: the supplied 411-page PDF was generated with Calibre in 2023 and bears third-party OceanofPDF watermarks; bibliographic details were confirmed internally.

## Bottom line

Do not accept “we need a course” as the problem definition. Start with a measurable organizational result, identify the specific observable actions that contribute to it, diagnose why each priority action is not happening, and select the simplest combination of environmental and learning interventions. When practice is needed, design realistic decisions and consequences before adding the minimum necessary information.

Action mapping is not a mind map for organizing content. It is a dependency map that forces every proposed activity, aid and piece of information to justify its relationship to performance and the goal. Its business-goal framing is useful but requires causal humility: training may contribute to a metric without being solely responsible for it.

## Action-map dependency chain

```text
organizational problem and measure
  → measurable goal
    → priority observable job actions
      → causes and barriers for each action
        → environmental remedies and/or practice
          → minimum required information and support
            → prototype evidence
              → delivery in workflow
                → behavior and outcome evaluation
```

Nothing enters the solution because it is interesting, traditional or already available as slides. Each item must support a priority action or remove a diagnosed barrier. Keep assumptions visible and update the map when evidence changes.

## Request-reframing protocol

When asked to build training, avoid immediately agreeing, rejecting the request or lecturing the sponsor. Establish shared purpose:

1. What observable problem or opportunity prompted the request?
2. Who is affected, and whose behavior is expected to change?
3. What evidence shows the current state?
4. Which organizational measure should move if the problem improves?
5. What has already been tried, and what happened?
6. What deadline, mandate, risk or constraint shapes the request?
7. Can we spend a bounded session mapping the simplest effective response?

Treat sponsor and SME claims as inputs, not facts. Include recent or prospective performers where possible because experts often interpret a tool or procedure as easier than novices do. For regulatory or awareness mandates where behavior and outcome cannot be fully specified, record the mandate honestly rather than inventing a causal business goal.

## Measurable goal contract

The source proposes a form such as: “A measure we already use will increase/decrease from or by an amount by a date.” Govern it with a fuller record:

- baseline, target, time window and owner;
- population, process and context in scope;
- why the measure matters and to whom;
- data source, quality, privacy and access;
- leading indicators closer to the target actions;
- other interventions and external influences;
- expected contribution, not unsupported attribution;
- review date and conditions for revising the project.

A business outcome can align the work without proving causality. Pair lagging results such as incidents, revenue or retention with nearer measures such as adoption, error pattern, response quality or task completion. Do not optimize a proxy that harms people or the underlying purpose.

## Observable-action standard

Ask: “What must people do on the job for the goal to become more likely?” A usable action:

- occurs in the actual role, not only in a test;
- begins with a specific behavior;
- identifies the situation or trigger when necessary;
- includes a quality, speed, frequency or safety standard;
- is narrow enough to diagnose and practice;
- can be observed through ethical evidence;
- makes its relationship to the goal plausible.

“Understand the policy,” “know the techniques” and “be supportive” are not adequate job actions. Ask what a competent person visibly does differently. Organize broad actions into specific sub-actions, then prioritize roughly five to ten based on consequence, frequency, difficulty and contribution. Required facts can be added later as enabling information.

## Barrier-analysis router

For each priority action ask, “Why is this not happening correctly now?” or, for a new behavior, “What could make this difficult?” Start with the environment when stakeholders are already assuming ignorance.

| Cause | Questions | Candidate responses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environment | Are tools, access, time, workload, process, incentives, management, culture or physical/emotional conditions obstructing the action? | Repair tools/processes, change workload or incentives, clarify ownership, improve access, provide job aids |
| Skill | Can people perform the action reliably in relevant conditions, or does it require practice and judgment? | Modeling, realistic practice, feedback, coaching, spaced rehearsal |
| Knowledge | Is essential information missing, inaccurate, hard to find or unavailable at the point of need? | Concise reference, searchable aid, embedded guidance, necessary explanation and retrieval where memorization matters |
| Motivation | Do people see value, expect success and experience coherent consequences? Is “motivation” actually created by the environment? | Resolve conflicting incentives and trust issues, reduce cost, provide agency/support; avoid blame and coercion |

Causes usually interact. A software defect cannot be trained away, and a job aid cannot build an interpersonal judgment skill by itself. Record solutions beside the relevant action and assign environmental changes to owners with authority to make them.

## Intervention portfolio

Choose the smallest responsible portfolio rather than one format for everything:

- process, policy, interface or tool correction;
- manager action, workload or incentive change;
- checklist, decision tree, template or searchable reference;
- guided observation, mentoring or coached work;
- short scenario or simulation near the point of need;
- spaced practice challenges over time;
- live discussion for ambiguity, controversy or collaborative judgment;
- structured course only when a coherent sequence and sustained facilitation are genuinely required.

Select timing and medium per action. A reminder may belong inside the workflow; a difficult conversation may need rehearsal before use and reflection afterward. A live event should prioritize practice, dialogue and debrief—not become the container for every asset.

## Practice-activity specification

Brainstorm activities before information. A strong activity:

1. practices a priority job action;
2. presents a credible situation containing the pressures and incomplete signals of work;
3. requires a meaningful decision or performance;
4. makes common errors tempting for realistic reasons;
5. reveals plausible consequences;
6. provides specific feedback and another chance to improve;
7. permits appropriate job aids that will exist in real performance;
8. varies or spaces later practice when durability matters.

Do not call recall questions “practice” when the job requires diagnosis, selection, communication or execution. Conversely, do not build a complex scenario when a simple checklist at the point of use solves the problem.

## SME interview protocol

For one representative high-priority action, ask the SME:

- What exactly triggers the decision?
- What does competent action look like, step by step?
- What makes this difficult in reality?
- What are the most common mistakes and why do capable people make them?
- What clues distinguish this case from similar cases?
- What happens after each plausible choice?
- Where do experts disagree or use judgment?
- What information or tool is actually available at that moment?
- Can you recall a de-identified real example?

Use multiple SMEs for consequential or contested work and distinguish policy from personal preference. Protect confidential cases and check legal, clinical, safety or regulatory claims with appropriate authority.

## Scenario decision-point pattern

### Stem

Set a concise, specific situation and ask what the person should do. Include only details that shape the decision. Use direct, natural language and relevant urgency; avoid fake drama, decorative character biographies and clues that make the answer obvious.

### Options

Use the best available action plus plausible mistakes gathered from real work. Make incorrect options attractive for authentic reasons rather than grammatically or morally absurd. Avoid “all/none of the above,” trivia, trick wording and caricature.

### Feedback

Continue the story to show a realistic consequence unique to the choice, then provide concise explanatory feedback or a reference. Make the causal chain proportionate: not every imperfect choice creates catastrophe. In high-stakes simulations, clearly separate fictional consequences from actual procedures and ensure correction.

### Branching

Use branching only when choices genuinely alter later conditions and recovery from mistakes is itself important. Sketch endings and paths before scripting, allow recovery where realistic, and include debrief questions for ambiguity. Complexity must serve judgment, not entertainment.

## Minimum-information rule

After drafting the activity, identify only the information necessary to make or learn from the decision. Decide whether it must be memorized or can be available in the environment. Prefer a job aid that learners consult during practice when that aid will exist at work. Reinforce relevant fragments in feedback.

Information may be required before an activity when lack of it would make the attempt meaningless or unsafe. “Pull, don't push” is a useful default, not an absolute. Safety instructions, informed consent, prerequisite concepts and operating constraints may need explicit orientation first.

## Prototype and production loop

Prototype one typical decision point before writing the full program. Test separately with sponsor, SMEs and representative performers:

- Does it reproduce a real decision and common pressure?
- Are the options genuinely plausible and distinct?
- Is the intended consequence credible and proportionate?
- What interpretations or accessibility barriers appear?
- Can learners use the expected aid or tool?
- Is the challenge too easy, too obscure or based on missing prerequisites?

Revise, then approve a concise project charter covering the goal, priority actions, action map, all training and non-training remedies, prototypes, sequence, owners, delivery timing and evaluation. Produce activities in small batches and review them rather than approving a huge script upfront.

## Evaluation and attribution chain

Evaluate the whole intervention portfolio, not only the learning asset:

1. usability, relevance and accessibility;
2. performance within representative activities;
3. delayed and varied performance;
4. use of the target action at work;
5. movement in nearer process indicators;
6. movement in the organizational goal;
7. harms, gaming, workload and unintended effects;
8. contribution of training versus tools, management changes and external factors.

Participant satisfaction is diagnostic feedback, not the success criterion. Compare baseline and follow-up where feasible and revise the causal map when actions improve but outcomes do not—or outcomes improve without the predicted actions.

## Evidence, scope and ethics limitations

- The method is a practitioner framework; the book does not provide comparative trials of action mapping as a complete system.
- Its critique of workplace “information dumps” should not be generalized into dismissing knowledge, conceptual instruction or formal education.
- Business metrics can be distant, noisy and vulnerable to gaming; a stated goal does not prove the behavioral causal chain.
- Learner actions alone cannot fix unsafe workloads, discriminatory systems, defective products or harmful policy.
- Motivation analysis can become blame; investigate incentives, power, trust and feasibility first.
- Fictional choices and consequences can oversimplify contested professional judgment or encode one SME's bias.
- Scenario realism must not expose identifiable clients, employees, patients or confidential business information.
- Technology and website recommendations date from 2017 and require current security, privacy, accessibility and availability review.
- A readability score is one signal, not a guarantee of comprehension, accessibility or respectful language.

## VladOS applications

- **Corporate workshops:** require an intervention brief and action map before agreeing to a workshop format.
- **LinkedIn training:** link business or professional goals to ethical behaviors such as research, profile evidence, useful contribution and consent-respecting conversation; never optimize spam volume.
- **Executive coaching:** map client-owned outcomes and behaviors while keeping employer metrics, confidentiality and clinical boundaries explicit.
- **O!Sapiens/Sapiens OS:** separate education, self-management skills, environmental supports and clinician-owned decisions.
- **Psychedelic integration education:** do not use performance mapping to imply clinical efficacy or readiness; prioritize consent, confidentiality, referral and non-directive meaning-making.
- **AI-supported design:** use AI to propose actions, barriers and scenarios only as hypotheses; require human domain review, source checks, privacy controls and representative testing.
