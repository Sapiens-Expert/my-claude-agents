## Bottom line

Practical writing succeeds only when a reader notices it, understands it, finds it relevant and can respond with tolerable effort. Busy readers skim, scan, postpone and stop; these are rational adaptations to scarce attention, not reader defects. The writer should therefore minimize unnecessary load, reveal priority through structure, make relevance truthful, and reduce action friction. VladOS should adopt the six principles as an **attention-to-action diagnostic**, governed by completeness, informed choice, accessibility, identity equity and anti-manipulation controls.

## Foundational model: the attention-to-action path

A practical message must clear five gates:

1. **Notice:** does the intended reader recognize that the communication may matter?
2. **Engage:** does expected value justify the apparent time and effort?
3. **Navigate:** can a skimming/scanning reader find the relevant part and reconstruct the message correctly?
4. **Understand and care:** is the request/idea clear, credible and legitimately relevant?
5. **Act:** are the required steps, information, choices and timing feasible?

Failure at a later gate cannot compensate for failure earlier. Measure each gate separately: delivery/open or first attention, comprehension, relevance/trust, start, completion and correct outcome. Do not treat clicks or completion alone as proof of understanding or welfare.

## The six principles as governed skills

### 1. Less is more — information-budget protocol

1. State the one highest-priority understanding and, if applicable, one primary action.
2. Delete words that add no meaning; replace inflated phrases with common direct forms.
3. Separate necessary from useful and merely interesting ideas.
4. Remove or defer secondary requests; split messages when timing/audience permits.
5. Estimate total reader cost across all recipients, follow-ups and channels—not word count alone.
6. Restore anything required for safety, informed consent, accurate qualification, relational warmth, accessibility or legal duty.
7. Test concise and fuller variants on comprehension, trust, action quality and equity—not response volume alone.

The book reports useful field evidence: a 49-word request to 7,002 school-board members produced a 4.8% survey response versus 2.7% for 127 words; a shorter newsletter generated roughly twice as many resource-link clicks; other school-family experiments also favored reduced content. These demonstrate contextual effects, not a universal optimal length.

### 2. Make reading easy — comprehension-load audit

- Replace rare or institutional words with familiar equivalents unless precision would be lost.
- Define necessary technical terms at first use and keep labels consistent.
- Prefer subject-verb-object order; make logical relationships explicit.
- Break nested clauses and long dependency chains, while preserving relationships among claims.
- Vary sentence length; use a short sentence for a critical instruction, not as a universal style.
- Evaluate readability with humans; grade-level formulas inspect surface features and cannot establish comprehension.
- Test with multilingual readers, low-literacy readers, cognitive disabilities, screen readers and realistic time pressure when material.

The goal is minimum **comprehension effort**, not minimum syllables. Familiarity, syntax, prior knowledge, typography, layout and motivation interact.

### 3. Design for easy navigation — scan-path architecture

1. Put the purpose, consequence, deadline or primary action where it is immediately visible.
2. Separate distinct topics visually and semantically.
3. Keep related information together, especially action plus date, location, eligibility and link.
4. Order by reader priority rather than writer chronology or organizational hierarchy.
5. Write descriptive headings that answer the reader's likely question.
6. Use tables, diagrams or graphics only when they reduce search/comparison effort; provide accessible equivalents.
7. Run a scan test: expose the artifact briefly, then ask readers what matters, to whom, by when and what happens next.

The strongest example is a 17-month experiment involving nearly 324,000 New York City court summonses: a redesigned form making appearance purpose/details prominent and grouped reduced failures to appear by 13%. The bundled redesign does not identify the isolated effect of any single feature.

### 4. Use enough formatting but no more — attention-allocation budget

- Establish what each formatting convention means in the channel and audience.
- Use semantic headings/lists before visual emphasis.
- Bold, underline or highlight only information that deserves displaced attention.
- Assume unformatted material will receive less attention after a salient highlight.
- If many items appear critical, re-evaluate priority or split the communication.
- Avoid all-caps blocks: they are slower to read, may reduce comprehension and are often interpreted as shouting.
- Do not rely only on color, styling, position or visual contrast; preserve programmatic semantics and plain-text meaning.

In one online study (`N=1,662`), formatting an embedded instruction increased compliance from 65% to 89% and reduced reading time. Another experiment showed irrelevant highlighting reduced discovery of an unformatted instruction; a third found one highlight outperformed five (84% versus 65% compliance). These controlled tasks demonstrate attention allocation, not every real-world formatting context.

### 5. Tell readers why they should care — truthful relevance bridge

Answer two questions early:

- **So what?** Which authentic outcome, value, cost, opportunity or duty connects the content to the reader?
- **Why me?** Which precise condition makes this relevant to this recipient or subgroup?

Use actual reader research and segment knowledge rather than stereotypes. A Rock the Vote field test sent recruitment emails to 19,990 people; framing the subject around free event access produced nearly four times the volunteer sign-ups of a writer-goal frame. The benefit was real, but the example also exposes a boundary: a relevance hook that causes uninterested recipients to open can waste time or feel deceptive. State the exchange and material conditions plainly.

### 6. Make responding easy — ethical response-friction map

Map the action as the reader experiences it:

1. **Steps:** remove redundant logins, fields, handoffs, searches and channel switches.
2. **Information:** colocate eligibility, cost, deadline, duration, required documents, destination and confirmation.
3. **Choice:** present a manageable, meaningful set; explain differences instead of merely reducing options.
4. **Attention:** prefill only verified data, provide direct links, chunk the task, save progress and confirm completion.
5. **Timing:** send when action is possible; use proportionate reminders that add value and allow opt-out.
6. **Recovery:** enable correction, undo, alternatives, accessibility support and human help.
7. **Autonomy:** defaults and preselection require transparent consequences, easy refusal and alignment with the person's interests.

Friction can reveal a broken process that writing cannot repair. Never use simplification to accelerate an action whose risks, price, data use or alternatives are obscured.

## Chapter 10 operational questions

| Situation | Decision rule |
| --- | --- |
| Multiple important pieces | Verify they share audience, timing and action; otherwise split. If bundled, summarize priorities and use navigable sections. |
| Multiple communications | Design a sequence with a declared purpose and cadence; each message should add value and preserve context. |
| Reminders | Remind close enough to action, identify what remains, use respectful frequency and stop after completion/opt-out. |
| Technical language | Use when audience precision requires it; define, layer or link supporting detail rather than forcing all readers through it. |
| Mixed audiences | Segment where lawful and feasible; otherwise lead with shared information and provide labeled audience-specific paths. |
| Timing | Match send time to relevance, ability to act, urgency and channel norms; avoid manufacturing urgency. |
| Messenger | Use a recognizable, credible and accountable sender; disclose sponsorship and never fabricate authority or identity. |
| Channel | Choose based on urgency, sensitivity, expected response, accessibility, persistence and reader preference—not fashion. |
| Hyperlinks | Use descriptive link text, disclose destination/action, minimize disruptive branching and keep critical facts outside the link. |
| Humor, sarcasm, emoji | Use only with high contextual confidence and low consequence; ambiguity grows across age, culture, status and text-only channels. |
| Pictures | Prefer when they improve recognition, comparison or procedure; add text alternatives and never substitute decoration for instruction. |

## Identity, equity and the truth test

The same message may receive different responses depending on the perceived gender, race, status, role or institutional identity of its writer. The book cites randomized audit evidence of lower response to writers believed to be Black and bias in professional evaluation. It also notes a double bind: warmth, apology and qualification may be demanded more from women or lower-status writers, while directness may be penalized.

VladOS response:

- diagnose discriminatory response patterns at the system level rather than “fixing” marginalized writers;
- test outcomes across relevant groups while minimizing sensitive data and protecting privacy;
- distinguish context-appropriate register from coerced stereotype performance;
- allow relational warmth when it serves trust, not as surplus automatically cut by brevity;
- use standardized review, response commitments and blind evaluation where appropriate;
- document who bears the time and emotional cost of communication norms.

Apply the **truth test**: Is the stated purpose the actual purpose? Are key facts prominent in proportion to their decision importance? Complexity, formatting or length must never be used to bury compensation, cost, risk, privacy, consent, cancellation or uncertainty. Deliberate obfuscation is not effective writing.

## Busy-reader production workflow

### Brief

- Reader(s), context, channel and device
- Writer goal and reader goal; conflicts between them
- One essential understanding; one primary action
- Relevance statement: “so what / why me / why now”
- Required facts, qualifications, consent and safety content
- Response steps, alternatives and recovery
- Evidence and success/failure measures

### Draft

Write the essential message first. Add necessary context in priority order. Group action information, use descriptive headings, and choose the simplest accurate vocabulary. Add warmth, explanation or evidence when the relationship and stakes require them.

### Three-pass edit

1. **Subtraction:** remove surplus words, ideas, requests and formatting.
2. **Navigation:** test first view, headings, grouping, order and scan path.
3. **Action and integrity:** verify relevance, sender, dates, links, friction, consent, risk, privacy, accessibility and truth.

### Five-second and action tests

Show a representative reader the artifact briefly, then ask:

- What is this about?
- Is it for you, and why?
- What matters most?
- What must happen, by when?
- What would you do next?

Then have them complete the action in realistic conditions. Record comprehension, time, errors, abandonment, trust, support need and group differences. A/B tests should measure correct outcomes and harms, not only opens or conversions.

## Evidence assessment

- Evidence ranges from large randomized field experiments and peer-reviewed studies to online surveys, anecdotes and untested rewrites. Preserve that distinction in every downstream claim.
- Several original studies use MTurk/Prolific or professional-class samples. Sample composition, incentives and artificial reading tasks constrain generalization.
- Multi-feature redesigns demonstrate package effects but cannot assign causality to each principle.
- Response rate, click rate and speed are partial outcomes. They may trade off against comprehension, trust, deliberation, accuracy or long-term relationship quality.
- Behavioral mechanisms such as present bias, selective attention and choice overload are context-dependent; defaults and “nudges” can redistribute welfare and power.
- Some research cited was current through 2023. Channel norms, accessibility standards and platform behavior need current verification before operational use.
- The authors acknowledge contextual limits and cases where warmth or completeness justifies length; this is central, not an exception to ignore.
