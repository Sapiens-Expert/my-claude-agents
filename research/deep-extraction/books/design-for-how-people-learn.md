# Design for How People Learn

## Bibliographic record

- Julie Dirksen, *Design for How People Learn*.
- New Riders, 2012. ISBN-13 978-0-321-76843-8; ISBN-10 0-321-76843-4.
- Coverage: introduction, all ten chapters, conclusion, references and index.
- Authority: evidence-informed practitioner guide that translates cognitive psychology and behavior-design concepts into instructional-design decisions. It is not a systematic review or a current evidence guideline.
- Source caveat: the supplied 294-page PDF was generated with Calibre in 2020; the internal copyright record identifies the first edition.

## Bottom line

Learning design starts with a performance problem, not a content request. Before creating instruction, determine whether the gap arises from **knowledge, skill, motivation, environment or communication**. Training can address some knowledge and skill gaps; the other causes commonly require clearer expectations, better tools, different incentives, prompts, practice conditions or process redesign.

The book's strongest transferable system is: understand the learner and context, specify observable performance, design for memory and attention, make practice resemble use, supply feedback, and support performance in the environment. Its metaphors and selected behavioral claims are useful prompts rather than settled mechanisms; claims from this 2012 edition require contemporary verification before high-stakes use.

## Performance-gap router

Treat a request for a course or workshop as a hypothesis. Ask what people currently do, what they should do, how the difference was observed, and what conditions surround both states.

| Gap | Diagnostic question | Likely response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Do people lack facts, concepts, rules or orientation needed for the task? | Explanation, examples, reference material and retrieval practice |
| Skill | Could a person reasonably become proficient without practice? | Progressive practice, feedback, variation and supported transfer |
| Motivation | Can they perform but do not start, persist or prioritize it? | Investigate value, confidence, incentives, tradeoffs, norms and consequences |
| Environment | Do tools, time, access, workflow, defaults or competing demands obstruct performance? | Job aids, resource/process redesign, prompts and removal of friction |
| Communication | Was the expectation, change or requirement actually made clear and credible? | Clarify message, ownership, timing, channel and confirmation |

These causes can coexist. Validate them with observation, interviews, work samples and performance data. A self-report such as “they need training” is not diagnosis. When the environment rewards the old behavior or makes the new one impractical, instruction alone transfers responsibility unfairly to the learner.

## Learner-context profile

Profile the learner only to the granularity needed for design:

- desired outcome and reason for participating;
- relevant prior knowledge, skills and misconceptions;
- novice, competent and expert differences;
- task, tools, social setting, language, access and time constraints;
- consequences of error and available support;
- voluntary versus mandatory participation and relevant power asymmetries.

Use interviews, observation, representative artifacts, small prototypes and tests. Experts often compress steps into automatic chunks and may omit what novices need; observe both expert work and novice failure. Do not sort people into fixed “visual,” “auditory” or “kinesthetic” types. Multiple representations can improve access, meaning and practice, but the modality should fit the content and task rather than a presumed learner identity.

## Performance specification

Define the destination as what the learner must be able to **do**, under which conditions, to what standard, and with which permitted supports. Separate:

1. recognition of information;
2. recall without cues;
3. explanation or interpretation;
4. application in a familiar case;
5. adaptation in a novel or pressured case;
6. sustained use in the real environment.

Match assessment to the actual level. A multiple-choice recognition check cannot establish unaided recall, judgment or skilled performance. Estimate the journey from current capability to required proficiency; a short workshop can begin a complex skill but should not claim mastery.

## Memory and retrieval design

The book distinguishes sensory input, limited working memory and long-term memory. Use that model as a design approximation:

- remove irrelevant information and decorative detail;
- organize new material into meaningful chunks and visible frameworks;
- connect it to accurate prior knowledge;
- use contrasting examples and counterexamples to refine concepts;
- require learners to retrieve, explain, choose and perform;
- revisit learning over time rather than relying on one concentrated exposure;
- vary contexts while preserving the cues that will exist at the point of use;
- provide external reference material when memorization has no operational value.

Familiarity is not recall. Re-reading or seeing an answer can create a sense of knowing while leaving performance fragile. Practice the retrieval and decision that the real task requires. Stories can provide causal and contextual structure, but vividness is not truth; verify the claim independently and do not let narrative replace procedure or practice.

## Attention and relevance integrity

Attention is selective and habituates. Gain it by connecting the learning to a real decision, problem or consequence. Questions, appropriate surprise, social interaction, play, visuals and stories may help, but each device must serve the outcome.

Apply four checks:

1. **Relevance:** does it help the learner notice or perform what matters?
2. **Truth:** is the urgency, uncertainty or emotional framing accurate?
3. **Load:** does it clarify the signal or compete with it?
4. **Agency:** can the learner participate without coerced disclosure, humiliation or manufactured fear?

Prefer meaningful task tension over decorative stimulation. Extrinsic rewards can redirect attention toward the prize and should not be treated as a universal motivation tool.

## Knowledge design and CCAF

For conceptual knowledge, discover what learners already believe and where their mental model breaks. Provide an organizing frame, then use examples, boundary cases and misconception correction. Guidance should decline as capability grows.

The book's CCAF pattern is a useful activity specification:

- **Context:** a credible situation and role;
- **Challenge:** a decision or problem of appropriate difficulty;
- **Activity:** the learner does the relevant cognitive or physical work;
- **Feedback:** timely information about the result, reasoning and next adjustment.

CCAF is a design prompt, not proof that learning occurred. The context must represent the task, the challenge must expose the target judgment, the activity must belong to the learner, and feedback must be specific enough to improve the next attempt.

## Skills practice ladder

If proficiency cannot be acquired without practice, design a sequence rather than a lecture:

1. observe a modeled performance with key decisions made visible;
2. attempt a bounded component with guidance;
3. combine components in a realistic case;
4. repeat with feedback and correction;
5. vary cases, constraints and cues;
6. withdraw scaffolding progressively;
7. perform under real or safely simulated conditions;
8. revisit after delay and support transfer in the workplace.

Keep difficulty high enough to require effort but low enough to make useful correction possible. Feedback should describe what happened, why it matters and what to try next. Avoid public ranking where it threatens psychological safety or distorts the desired behavior.

## Motivation and behavior design

Knowing is not doing. Diagnose whether the learner values the outcome, believes it is feasible, can see how to begin, and receives reinforcement from the environment. Useful design options include credible modeling, small successful attempts, choice, progress visibility, social support and reducing practical barriers.

The book discusses self-efficacy, adoption categories, social proof, opinion leaders and reinforcement. Treat these as hypotheses to test in context. Never manufacture consensus, exploit authority, shame slower adopters or hide material costs. For durable change, pair instruction with selected actions, clear cues, sufficient ability, environmental support and review.

## Environment and performance support

Do not force memory when the environment can safely carry the information. Use checklists, decision trees, templates, worked examples, searchable references, interface guidance or prompts when:

- information changes frequently;
- the task is infrequent or high consequence;
- exact steps or thresholds matter;
- cognitive load is already high;
- external support is available at the point of need.

Audit the full path: access, permissions, time, tools, handoffs, defaults, incentives, manager behavior and consequences. A reminder cannot fix missing authority or an impossible workload. Remove structural friction before adding motivational messaging.

## Evaluation chain

Separate five questions:

1. Did participants find the experience acceptable and usable?
2. Can they demonstrate the intended knowledge or skill?
3. Can they do so after a meaningful delay?
4. Do they use it in the real context?
5. Does that behavior contribute to the intended result without unacceptable harm?

Track relevant supports and confounders. Completion, satisfaction and content coverage are weak proxies for transfer. Use the findings to revise diagnosis, practice, support and workflow—not merely the presentation.

## Evidence and currency limitations

- Published in 2012, the book predates substantial later research and contemporary accessibility, privacy and AI-mediated learning conditions.
- It is a broad practitioner synthesis; individual studies and generalizations are not appraised systematically and effect sizes are not established.
- Memory-system diagrams, the Rider/Elephant metaphor and similar models simplify contested or more complex mechanisms.
- Claims related to willpower as a limited resource or ego depletion require current revalidation and must not support moral judgments about learners.
- The rejection of fixed learning-style matching is directionally appropriate; multimodal and accessible design should still respond to content, disability, language and context.
- Emotional arousal, urgency, competition, social proof and reward design are dual-use and require truth, proportionality, consent and harm checks.
- Educational design must not be represented as diagnosis or treatment of clinical, neurological or learning conditions.

## VladOS applications

- **Corporate workshops:** diagnose the performance system before accepting a training brief; include manager and workflow support in transfer plans.
- **LinkedIn education:** practice drafting, evaluating and revising profiles, posts and messages in a sandbox; do not require live publishing or unsolicited contact.
- **Executive coaching:** use educational materials and practice aids without treating them as individualized clinical assessment.
- **O!Sapiens/Sapiens OS:** separate factual education, coached behavior practice, environmental supports and medical referral.
- **Psychedelic integration education:** use clear scope, consent, sober participation, confidentiality and referral; never treat learning activities as therapy or evidence of readiness.
- **AI-supported learning:** let AI provide bounded examples and feedback while protecting confidential data, checking accuracy and preserving human review for consequential judgment.
