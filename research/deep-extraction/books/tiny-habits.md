# Tiny Habits — BJ Fogg

## Bibliographic record

- *Tiny Habits: The Small Changes That Change Everything*; BJ Fogg, PhD; Houghton Mifflin Harcourt; first edition; 2020.
- Hardcover ISBN 978-0-358-00332-8; ebook ISBN 978-0-358-00399-1.
- One 357-page English PDF; SHA-256 `73d3b14545293a5e4c29d8b32cb48a3ec1ce049382948984d315f54b9107e751`; about 99,098 extracted words; no OCR required.
- Complete coverage: introduction, all eight chapters, conclusion, acknowledgments, Behavior Model/Tiny Habits appendices, Behavior Change Masterplan, celebration inventory and 300 example recipes.
- Authority: an author-originated practitioner framework combining behavior-science concepts, teaching/coaching experience, self-experimentation and participant stories. The book reports extensive use of the method but does not provide a peer-reviewed trial of the complete branded system.

## Bottom line

The Fogg Behavior Model says a behavior occurs when motivation, ability and a prompt converge at the same moment:

```text
B = MAP
Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt
```

The Tiny Habits recipe applies this model through:

```text
After I [existing anchor], I will [tiny behavior].
Then I will [immediate authentic celebration].
```

The strongest contribution is diagnostic: when behavior does not occur, troubleshoot prompt and feasibility before blaming motivation or character. VladOS retains this principle but treats B=MAP as a practical design model, not a universal quantitative equation. The goal must be person-owned or legitimately co-designed, and all interventions remain subject to consent, benefit, safety, accessibility, privacy and the right to stop.

## Governed Behavior Design model

### Step 0 — Establish legitimacy

Before designing a behavior, record:

- whose aspiration it is and whether participation is voluntary;
- expected human benefit and supporting evidence;
- who requests the change and who bears effort, risk or surveillance;
- structural constraints, disability/accessibility needs and available resources;
- whether the behavior involves health, addiction, finance, legal rights, employment, children or another high-stakes area;
- alternatives, including system change and no change; and
- review, stop, referral and redress routes.

Fogg's first maxim—help people do what they already want to do—is a valuable floor, not complete consent. A desire can be manufactured, conflicted, misinformed or constrained. Confirm comprehension, freedom, authority and welfare.

### Step 1 — Translate aspiration into candidate behaviors

The source distinguishes abstract aspirations/outcomes from concrete actions. Use a behavior swarm to generate options across:

- one-time setup or removal;
- recurring action;
- environment/access change;
- skill or information;
- social support;
- policy, workflow or resource change; and
- protective friction or deliberate non-action.

Avoid solving every problem with an individual routine. Include actions available to managers, institutions, product owners and policymakers when they control the constraint.

### Step 2 — Select a responsible “golden behavior”

The source's three criteria are effectiveness, desire and feasibility. VladOS adds evidence and integrity:

| Test | Question |
| --- | --- |
| relevance | Does it plausibly contribute to the chosen aspiration? |
| willingness | Does the person genuinely want this specific behavior? |
| feasibility | Can it happen under low/ordinary motivation and real conditions? |
| evidence | What supports the mechanism and what remains uncertain? |
| safety | Are risks, contraindications and competence requirements addressed? |
| equity | Who cannot do it, pays more, or is disadvantaged? |
| autonomy | Can the person refuse, revise and stop without penalty? |

Focus Mapping can compare impact and feasibility visually. Its placement is a current hypothesis, not proof; test in lived context.

### Step 3 — Diagnose B=MAP precisely

For one behavior at one moment:

```text
Behavior: actor + action + context + frequency/quality
Motivation: wants, conflicts, meaning, affect, incentives, social pressure
Ability: time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine fit, skill, access
Prompt: source, timing, visibility, precision, overload, controllability
Outcome: immediate and delayed benefit/burden
```

The source's Ability Chain directs attention to the weakest simplicity factor. Add language, executive function, disability, pain, fatigue, caregiving, digital access and psychological safety. “Easy” is person- and context-specific.

Troubleshoot in this order for low-risk chosen behavior:

1. Was there a clear prompt at a workable moment?
2. Was the action genuinely feasible?
3. Was this the right behavior and outcome?
4. Were motivation, ambivalence or competing needs decisive?
5. Is a structural, relational or clinical factor outside the model dominant?

### Step 4 — Start safely small

A **starter step** begins the sequence; **scaling back** reduces quantity or duration. Define:

- minimum version that still represents honest progress;
- optional expansion, never required in the moment;
- competence/safety floor that cannot be reduced;
- completion cue;
- maximum version preventing overextension; and
- recovery version after disruption.

Tiny is inappropriate when partial execution creates false reassurance—for example incomplete safety checks, token consent, inadequate medication dosing, superficial safeguarding or a misleading “minimum” professional standard.

### Step 5 — Choose a respectful prompt

The source distinguishes person prompts, context prompts and action prompts. It calls a reliable existing action an **anchor** and recommends its precise trailing edge.

Prompt quality test:

- naturally adjacent in place, frequency and purpose;
- noticeable but not interruptive;
- user-controlled and easy to disable;
- not tied to distress, shame, bodily urgency or sensitive disclosure without explicit justification;
- no exposure of private goals to others;
- compatible with accessibility and variable routines; and
- retired when no longer useful.

Notification volume is a system burden. Do not compensate for bad fit by escalating prompts.

### Step 6 — Reinforce without coercion

The source proposes immediate authentic celebration to create a feeling of success (“Shine”). VladOS broadens the mechanism claim: immediate positive affect and self-recognition may support repetition for some people; the book does not establish a universal neural-wiring rule.

Celebration must be:

- self-chosen, culturally and personally authentic;
- proportionate and private if preferred;
- attached to a safe action or learning, not mere compliance;
- free of deception, gambling-like variable rewards or public ranking;
- optional for people who find it awkward, dysregulating or incongruent; and
- distinct from evidence that the behavior produces the desired outcome.

Fogg's second maxim—help people feel successful—is useful when success is truthfully defined. Never manufacture success, praise unsafe effort, demand positivity or use rewards to override refusal.

### Step 7 — Review, grow, maintain or stop

Habits may expand naturally in duration/intensity or multiply into related practices. Growth is not required. Review:

- completion under ordinary conditions;
- benefit, burden, harm and opportunity cost;
- whether optional growth feels chosen;
- whether the anchor still fits;
- lapse/re-entry performance;
- identity effects and unwanted rigidity; and
- whether deliberate attention should replace automaticity.

Use a minimum viable version during difficult periods. Stop or redesign when the practice has no benefit, creates harm, crowds out priorities or becomes compulsive.

## Governed Tiny Habit recipe

```text
Aspiration and owner:
Chosen outcome/evidence:
Specific behavior:
Why this is the best current match:

After I [precise, reliable, appropriate anchor],
I will [minimum safe tiny behavior].
I may [optional expansion].
I will recognize success by [authentic optional response].

Ability/access support:
Privacy and prompt controls:
Recovery version:
Benefit/burden/harm measures:
Review and stop/referral conditions:
```

## Untangling unwanted patterns

The source's Behavior Change Masterplan sequences:

1. create helpful new habits;
2. stop a specific unwanted behavior by addressing prompt, ability or motivation; and
3. if needed, replace it with another behavior.

VladOS adds a classification/referral gate before this sequence. Break a vague “bad habit” into exact behaviors and contexts, start with the most tractable safe component, and treat lapses as data. Possible interventions are remove/change prompt, add protective friction, meet the underlying need, change environment, build support or substitute a safer response.

Do not try to make essential care, communication, eating, medication adherence or help-seeking harder without qualified oversight. The book explicitly says Tiny Habits is not the answer to serious addiction. Its uphill/downhill/freefall labels are explanatory metaphors, not diagnostic classes. Grief, eating patterns and other clinical concerns in anecdotes must not be generalized into self-treatment advice.

## Designing change with other people

Behavior Design creates a power issue whenever the designer and actor differ.

### Other-person design gate

1. Do you have legitimate authority or explicit invitation?
2. Has the person defined or accepted the outcome?
3. Are consequences of refusal transparent?
4. Can system owners change the environment/resources instead?
5. Are privacy, dignity, accessibility and unequal power protected?
6. Can the person revise the recipe and withdraw?
7. Is success a benefit to them, not convenience for the designer?

Parents, educators, managers, clinicians and product teams need role-specific safeguards. Do not diagnose nonperformance as low motivation, nag, infantilize adults, or engineer behavior covertly. Workplace “celebration” cannot substitute for pay, staffing, psychological safety or fair management.

## Product and marketing ethics

The model can make technology highly effective at eliciting behavior. Its dual-use risk is material. Prohibited uses include deceptive prompts, compulsive engagement, dark patterns, undisclosed surveillance, artificial urgency, hard-to-cancel flows, targeting vulnerability and rewards designed to maximize use irrespective of welfare.

For product use, add:

- explicit user benefit and success definition;
- minimum necessary prompting and data;
- healthy-use ceiling and satiation;
- transparent controls, undo, export and exit;
- protection for children and vulnerable users;
- monitoring of regret, distress, complaints and displacement; and
- independent review for consequential behavior.

The source acknowledges that behavior principles can be used for good or harm, but personal good intentions are not governance. Use enforceable product requirements and outcome measures.

## Evidence and claim discipline

- B=MAP is a conceptual relation and diagnostic prompt, not a calibrated mathematical equation.
- The “action line” is illustrative; the book does not supply a universal measured threshold.
- Motivation, ability and prompt are not necessarily independent, exhaustive or easily observed.
- “Information-action fallacy” is too absolute if read as information never changing behavior. Information can be necessary and sometimes sufficient; it often competes with other determinants.
- Claims that only epiphany, environment change or tiny habits create lasting change are not exhaustive.
- Starting tiny may improve feasibility, but bigger, urgent or coordinated changes can be necessary.
- “Emotions create habits” and celebration “wires” habits are stronger than the evidence presented for the complete technique.
- The reported 40,000-person coaching experience is not equivalent to a controlled effectiveness study; denominators, selection, attrition and measures need verification.
- Self-stories and selected transformations demonstrate feasibility, not average effect or causality.
- Identity shifts can support coherence but can also create rigidity, shame or group pressure; identity remains self-authored.
- Product, social-media and behavioral-technology examples are time-sensitive and need current verification.
- The branded method and examples should not be represented as clinical treatment or universal behavioral science.

## Relationships to VladOS foundations

- **The Power of Habit:** cue–routine–reward describes a recurring sequence; B=MAP asks why one action occurs now. Combine as complementary observation models, not a unified law.
- **Switch:** Tiny Habits develops “shrink the change” and Path design; Switch adds direction, emotion and system-level change legitimacy.
- **Nudge:** prompts, environment and ease are choice architecture. They inherit transparency, effective-autonomy, sludge and distributional controls.
- **Hooked:** prompts and reinforcement overlap triggers/rewards. Tiny Habits' stated person-owned outcome is a better default, while both require benefit, satiation and exit governance.
- **Scarcity:** reducing time/effort can respect bandwidth; do not use tiny actions to obscure missing resources or structural scarcity.
- **Mindset:** trial-and-adjust supports learning; avoid turning failed execution into an identity judgment or mandatory positivity.
- **Motivational interviewing:** MI should establish autonomous direction and work with ambivalence before behavior design specifies implementation.
- **COM-B/behavior-change sources:** D4 should compare opportunity/capability/motivation coverage and preserve social/structural determinants missing from a momentary B=MAP snapshot.
