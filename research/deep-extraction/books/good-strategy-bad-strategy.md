# Good Strategy/Bad Strategy

## Bibliographic record

- Author: Richard P. Rumelt.
- Full title: *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters*.
- Edition/year/language: first British edition, 2011, English; first published in the United States in 2011.
- Publisher/identifiers: Profile Books (UK); Crown Business/Random House (US); ISBN 978-1-84668-480-7; ebook ISBN 978-1-84765-746-6.
- Source paths and exact duplicates: two exact archived copies; SHA-256 `862eef8c2a5897030c443b5df9e2975b3e949b80392f1b632ce18a35f5af7109`.
- File completeness/extraction quality: 360 pages; complete introduction, 18 chapters, notes and index/contents material; good extraction; no OCR.
- Authority and intended audience: expert strategy text based on the author's academic research, teaching, consulting, cases, history and analytical argument. It is not a systematic empirical review, and many cases are retrospective interpretations selected to demonstrate strategic logic.

## Bottom line

- Central thesis: strategy is a coherent, reasoned response to an important challenge—not aspiration, financial goals, mission language, budgets, templates, or a long list of initiatives.
- Strongest contribution: the strategy kernel—diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action—combined with a practical taxonomy of bad strategy and multiple sources of strategic power.
- Main limitation: the author often infers strategy from successful historical cases; outcomes can support several interpretations, and analogies from war, firms, public institutions and national policy do not transfer automatically.
- Safe VladOS use: require a falsifiable diagnosis, explicit choices, a guiding approach, mutually reinforcing actions, resource concentration, assumptions, disconfirming evidence, owners and review dates. Preserve ethics, stakeholder effects, resilience and uncertainty rather than treating competitive advantage as the sole objective.

## Argument map

Rumelt begins with an obstacle-centered definition: good strategy recognizes what makes progress difficult and devises a focused way to overcome it. Because coordinated focus and fresh insight are uncommon, a coherent strategy can itself be an advantage. Bad strategy displaces this work through inflated language, avoidance of the real challenge, aspirations mislabeled as strategy, or objectives that are incoherent, impracticable, or disconnected from the diagnosis.

The kernel supplies the minimum logical structure. A diagnosis selects the critical structure of the situation. A guiding policy sets boundaries and an overall method for dealing with it. Coherent actions commit resources and create reinforcement. Part 2 then shows how this structure gains power: leverage, achievable proximate objectives, strengthening linked systems, design/fit, segment focus, capability-led growth, asymmetry, environmental dynamics, and active resistance to inertia and entropy.

Part 3 makes strategy epistemic rather than prophetic. A strategy is a hypothesis about what matters and how actions will work. It should be written, tested against alternatives, exposed to anomalies and history, and revised from results. Independent judgment requires resisting quick closure, social proof, internal narratives, and the belief that the present case is uniquely exempt from known failure patterns.

## The strategy kernel

### 1. Diagnosis

A diagnosis explains the nature of the challenge and reduces overwhelming complexity to a tractable causal structure. A useful diagnosis:

- names the obstacle rather than restating the desired outcome;
- distinguishes symptoms from plausible drivers;
- states scope, stakeholders, evidence, uncertainty and constraints;
- reveals what must be true for the diagnosis to hold;
- makes competing diagnoses and disconfirming observations visible.

### 2. Guiding policy

The guiding policy is the chosen overall approach. It channels action without pretending to specify every step. It should:

- address the diagnosed structure directly;
- define priorities, exclusions and decision rules;
- exploit or build relevant strengths;
- account for likely responses and change;
- remain specific enough to evaluate but flexible enough to adapt.

### 3. Coherent action

Actions instantiate the policy and support one another. A coherent action system identifies:

- a pivotal or proximate objective;
- resource commitments and actions deliberately stopped;
- owners, dependencies, sequencing and minimum capability;
- leading observations and outcome measures;
- review triggers, learning loops and exit/repair conditions.

## Bad-strategy diagnostic

| Failure mode | Detection question | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluff | Can abstract language be translated into observable choices or causal claims? | Replace slogans with plain diagnosis and action |
| Failure to face the challenge | What obstacle makes the aspiration difficult? | Research, prioritize and name the critical challenge |
| Goals mistaken for strategy | How will the obstacle be overcome, and why should this approach work? | Add guiding policy and coherent actions |
| Bad objectives | Are objectives mutually compatible, feasible, prioritized and strategy-derived? | Remove laundry lists; select pivotal/proximate objectives |
| Choice avoidance | What valued activity, segment or constituency will receive less? | Make trade-offs and consequences explicit |
| Template strategy | Did mission–values–goals fill blanks replace analysis? | Use templates only after diagnosis |
| Positive-thinking substitution | What could fail or contradict the thesis? | Add premortem, base rates and disconfirmation |

## Sources of strategic power

| Source | Operating logic | Failure condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leverage | Concentrate on a pivot where limited effort cascades | Wrong pivot, weak timing or insufficient force |
| Proximate objective | Solve the next feasible high-value constraint | Objective is remote, symbolic or below capability |
| Chain-link logic | Raise all binding links or redesign the system | Improving nonbinding links wastes resources |
| Design | Fit policies, capabilities and actions into a specialized whole | Fit becomes rigid or components conflict |
| Focus | Apply coordinated strength to the right segment/problem | Segment is too small, contested or changing |
| Advantage | Create value through asymmetry and capture part of it | Imitation, substitution, erosion or no buyer value |
| Dynamics | Anticipate and ride structural change | Trend extrapolation misses second-order effects |
| Anti-inertia | Change routine, culture or partner dependencies | Transformation ignores legitimate constraints |
| Anti-entropy | Maintain organizational purpose, boundaries and coordination | Unchecked proliferation and internal competition |

## Strategy as a learning system

1. Write the current diagnosis, not merely the plan.
2. List key assumptions and observable implications.
3. Generate at least one competing diagnosis and policy.
4. Use a create–destroy cycle: build the strongest case, then actively attack it.
5. Consult relevant expertise while retaining independent judgment.
6. Establish base rates, historical analogues, pre-mortem failure modes and stakeholder harms.
7. Choose the smallest decision or experiment that can resolve consequential uncertainty when feasible.
8. Precommit predictions and thresholds before results arrive.
9. Compare observation with expectation, capture anomalies and update the strategy.
10. Preserve decisions and superseded diagnoses in the decision log.

## Cases and interpretation

The book covers Apple, Desert Storm, Walmart, Cold War competition, Starbucks, Wells Fargo, Ford, Toyota, the Getty, Apollo, Challenger, GM, IKEA, Hannibal, Voyager, Paccar, Crown Cork & Seal, Telecom Italia, Disney, Cisco, newspapers, Continental, Nvidia, Global Crossing and the 2008 crisis. These cases reveal possible mechanisms and help reconstruct internal strategic logic. They are not controlled comparisons; selection, hindsight, survivor bias, incomplete stakeholder costs and later developments limit causal inference.

Military cases are especially useful for concentration, anticipation and coordination but differ from business and public-interest work. VladOS must not import enemy logic, deception, zero-sum assumptions, command hierarchy or acceptable-loss reasoning into coaching, health, community or ethical marketing.

- Provides the diagnostic spine for evaluating *Blue Ocean Strategy*: value innovation and market creation should be treated as possible guiding policies, not universal answers.
- Creates a standard for *Positioning* and *Play Bigger*: a category or mental position matters only if it addresses a diagnosed challenge and is backed by coherent capability and action.
- Frames brand strategy books: identity, equity and stories are resources or policies within strategy, not substitutes for it.
- Corrects [Expert Secrets](expert-secrets.md): “new opportunity,” movement and funnel tactics need diagnosis, real advantage, coherent action and disconfirming evidence; belief creation is not strategy.

## VladOS applications

- Strategy: require every project strategy to include diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action, non-actions, assumptions and review triggers.
- Brands: diagnose whether the main obstacle is awareness, relevance, trust, differentiation, evidence, distribution, offer fit or brand architecture before choosing messaging tactics.
- LinkedIn: distinguish strategic objectives and target relationships from posting frequency, tools, hooks and vanity metrics.
- Projects: select proximate objectives appropriate to current capabilities; build lower coordination rungs before expanding scope.
- Decisions: use written precommitment, competing hypotheses, outside view and create–destroy review.
- State: treat priority proliferation and conflicting projects as entropy requiring active pruning.
- AI governance: use AI to expand hypotheses and challenge logic, not to replace leadership choice or generate fluent strategic fluff.

No canonical framework or strategy file is changed from this book alone. The kernel is a strong candidate for later canonical synthesis after comparison with the prioritized strategy corpus.

## Limitations and do-not-infer

- Publication age: 2011; company positions, markets, media economics, technology and several explicit predictions are dated.
- Cases are primarily qualitative, retrospective and selected by the author; apparent coherence may partly be reconstructed after success.
- The framework does not fully specify stakeholder legitimacy, ethics, governance, measurement, execution cadence, portfolio management, uncertainty quantification or strategy emergence.
- Strategy can be pluralistic where organizations serve legitimate competing goals; focus does not erase legal duties, resilience, inclusion or public value.
- Competitive/military language can obscure cooperation, ecosystems, care, community and non-zero-sum value creation.
- “Advantage” must not justify exploitation, deception, regulatory arbitrage or avoidable harm.
- Do not infer that vision is useless, growth is bad, all large organizations lack strategy, every system has one weak link, tighter fit is always better, historical analogy predicts outcomes, or contrarian judgment is necessarily correct.
- A crisp kernel can still be built on a false diagnosis; evidence, learning and revision remain essential.
